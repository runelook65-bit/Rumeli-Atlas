# Rumelia Atlas research memo: Bosnia and Herzegovina

Bosnia and Herzegovina should be modeled as a **frontier province, corridor, and archive landscape**, not as a single homogeneous “Muslim” region. Ottoman administrative space changed repeatedly: early Bosnian, Herzegovinian, and Zvornik sanjaks; the Bosnian eyalet after c. 1580; later vilayet and kaza arrangements; and post-1878 Austro-Hungarian, Yugoslav, and present-day administrative geographies. The core atlas unit should therefore be a time-bounded place record linked to administrative, settlement, religious, linguistic, demographic, transport, and heritage evidence.

## Terms and identity safeguards

Use source terms exactly where they are meaningful: *sancak/sanjak*, *eyalet*, *vilayet*, *kaza*, *nahiye*, *mahalle/mahala*, *köy/selo*, *mezraa*, *reaya*, *timar*, *mukataa*, *muhacir*, *vakıf/waqf*, *vakıfname/waqfiyya*, *şer‘iyye sicili/sidžil*, *tahrir defteri*, *mufassal*, *icmal*, *evkaf*, *salname* and *kanunname*. Do not silently modernize Ottoman administrative terms into current municipal units.

Keep the following dimensions separate:

- **Muslim/Musliman** is primarily a religious or confessional category in Ottoman and later statistical sources. It is not automatically equivalent to the modern national category **Bošnjak/Bosniak**.
- **Bosniak/Bošnjak** is a modern national-affiliation term, although “Bosnian Muslim” appears in older scholarship and migration literature. Record the source’s wording rather than back-projecting “Bosniak” into every Ottoman entry.
- **Slavic Muslim** is a useful descriptive historical phrase for South-Slavic-speaking Muslims in some comparative works, but it is not a substitute for every use of Muslim, Bosniak, or local self-identification.
- **Albanian/Albanka** and **Turkish/Turčin/Turkinja** are distinct self-declared national-affiliation categories in the 2013 census. They should not be collapsed into Bosniak or Muslim.
- **Bosnian, Croatian, and Serbian** in the modern census are separate self-declared mother-tongue labels for closely related South Slavic varieties. They are not religions, and they should not be treated as direct proxies for ethnicity.
- Ottoman Turkish, Arabic, and Persian in a manuscript or document describe the language of the record or learned culture, not automatically the ethnicity or mother tongue of its author or subject.

The 2013 census explicitly defined *maternji jezik* as the language learned in early childhood or the language a respondent considered their mother tongue, with Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, and write-in options. Its tables separately enumerate ethnicity/national affiliation and religion; the total population was 3,531,159, including 1,769,592 Bosniaks, 1,086,733 Serbs, 544,780 Croats, 12,121 persons recorded as Muslim/Muslimanka, 2,659 Albanians, and 1,108 Turks. These are census self-identifications, not interchangeable historical population labels.[1] International monitoring also treated ethnicity, religion, and mother tongue as sensitive, open/self-declared questions and warned against political or methodological conflation.[2]

## Ottoman territorial and urban frame

The Turkish *İslâm Ansiklopedisi* entry is a useful first administrative scaffold. It describes the Bosnian sanjak after the Ottoman conquest, the creation of the Herzegovina sanjak in 1470, and a Zvornik-centered sanjak. Yayça/Jajce and Banaluka/Banja Luka were incorporated after the 1526 Battle of Mohács (1527 or 1528 in the entry). Sarajevo/Saraybosna grew as the Bosnian sanjak center through Gazi Husrev Bey’s külliye and endowments; the center later shifted to Banaluka through Ferhad Pasha’s construction, and the Bosnian eyalet was formed with seven sanjaks by about 1580.[3]

This sequence is important for the atlas: Sarajevo, Banja Luka, Travnik, Mostar, Tuzla, and Bihać were not simply six equivalent “Ottoman cities.” They occupied different positions in the province’s military and administrative network. Sarajevo was an institutional and commercial core; Banja Luka was a sanjak/eyalet seat and western frontier urbanization project; Travnik became the principal provincial capital in the later Ottoman period; Mostar was a Neretva crossing and Herzegovinian frontier town; Tuzla belonged to the northeastern Zvornik/Posavina network and to routes toward Sarajevo; and Bihać was a Habsburg-facing Krajina fortress and district center.

### Sarajevo / Saraybosna

Sarajevo is the best-documented local archive node. Gazi Husrev-beg Library reports **88 Sarajevo Sharia Court sijils** covering 1552–1565 and 1725–1852. The records contain information on citizens, professions, material status, books owned, legal transactions, and other aspects of political, cultural, and economic history. The same archive fund holds approximately 1,500 endowment charters, including 421 originals, along with thousands of other Ottoman documents and many Gazi Husrev-bey vakıf defters.[4]

For the atlas, Sarajevo’s urban layers should include the Gazi Husrev Bey külliye, *mahalle* and bazaar geography, court-record addresses, endowment property, craft and profession data, and routes over the surrounding passes. The 1800–1851 conversion study uses 48 Sarajevo sijils and stresses that Sarajevo is the only Bosnian location with a continuous enough run for this type of long-period analysis; other kazas survive much more fragmentarily.[5] This makes Sarajevo the methodological baseline, but not a safe demographic proxy for all Bosnia.

### Mostar

UNESCO describes Mostar as a settlement at a river and land-road crossing, with written evidence for a bridge in 1459 and the name Mostar first recorded in 1474. The historic town developed in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries as an Ottoman frontier town. Its urban morphology combines the bridge and towers, *mahalas*, arable land, houses, bazaar, water, and religious buildings. UNESCO explicitly describes the long coexistence of Muslims, Roman Catholic Croats, Eastern Orthodox Serbs, Sephardic Jews, and Bosniaks-Muslims, while treating these as distinct communities rather than a single identity.[6]

Mostar’s 2004 bridge reconstruction and the 7.60-hectare World Heritage property (with a 48-hectare buffer) must be modeled as both Ottoman urban heritage and post-war reconstruction history. The Gazi Husrev-beg Library’s Memory of the World entry includes a 1593 charter issued in Mostar, demonstrating that the city’s documentary record is not limited to architecture.[7] Search under *Mostar*, *Mostar/Mostari*, *Köprühisar* where encountered, *Neretva*, *Stari most*, *mahala*, and named vakıf institutions; do not assume that every “Turkish quarter” description is a primary Ottoman term.

### Travnik

Travnik should be treated as the later provincial-capital and archival node of central Bosnia. The Kantonalni arhiv Travnik is described as the principal public repository in Travnik, a city that was the capital of the Ottoman province of Bosnia for several centuries. HMML has catalogued 120 manuscripts from the archive: Arabic texts, Ottoman Turkish material, Persian items, and Bosnian authors and scribes. The dated range includes a manuscript copied in 1410 and a mosque account book covering 1869–1885.[8]

The account book is especially useful for linking manuscript evidence to vakıf and neighborhood history. A Travnik layer should combine the fortress, vizieral and administrative geography, mosque and *mahalle* records, local archive manuscripts, and the central-Bosnian road network. The city should not be represented only as a preserved Ottoman “townscape”; the archival evidence supports research into education, law, mosque finance, and provincial administration.

### Banja Luka / Banaluka

The TDV entry identifies Banaluka as the city to which the Bosnian administrative center shifted after Sarajevo’s growth, through Ferhad Pasha’s building activity.[3] Elma Korić’s study of Ferhad Pasha’s waqf provides a strong source-backed urbanization model. Ferhad Pasha’s late-sixteenth-century endowment funded a mosque, tomb, water supply, road communications, and other buildings; the author cites more than 216 public buildings constructed in Banja Luka between 1579 and 1587. The waqfiye survives in copies in Gazi Husrev-beg Library, the Kadić chronicle, and the Bašagić collection in Bratislava.[9]

Map the waqf as infrastructure, not only as a religious building: Ferhadija mosque, *turbe*, madrasa, palace, inns, bridge works, water, roads, and the Kastel fortress. The source describes Donji Šeher between the Vrbas and Crkvina streams and connects endowment income to maintenance, credit, education, and further capital works. Ferhad Pasha’s waqf therefore makes Banja Luka a key example of frontier urbanization through elite endowment.

### Tuzla

Tuzla should be modeled as a northeastern corridor node rather than assigned a generic “Ottoman city” label. Its precise Ottoman administrative status, toponymic history, salt economy, and changing relation to the Zvornik sanjak should be verified against the relevant tahrir, salname, and local archival entries before publication. A Bosnian study of Ottoman caravan roads in the Konjuh area documents connections among Kladanj, Zvornik, Olovo, Sarajevo, and Tuzla, showing that Tuzla belonged to a wider route and provisioning network rather than an isolated urban basin.[10]

The source reconstructs an 8.29-kilometer Kladanj–Tuholj–Miljkovac road segment and uses field archaeology, Austro-Hungarian maps, travel accounts, and Bosna vilayet salnames. This is a productive Tuzla-area research model: combine urban *mahalle* and waqf data with roads, passes, villages, mineral/salt evidence, market destinations, and modern GIS. Record “Tuzla” and any Ottoman variants found in the primary source separately until the toponym is verified.

### Bihać / Bihaç

Bihać belongs to the western Krajina and frontier-fortress layer. The TDV account places the Ottoman conquest of Bihać in the late sixteenth-century frontier sequence, following Hasan Pasha’s activity; use the source’s “Bihaç” spelling in Ottoman metadata and “Bihać” for the modern place record.[3] Bihać should be mapped through fortress, river, border, military provisioning, migration, and rural-settlement evidence, not only through surviving mosques.

Bihać is also important for migration research. Gelez notes that returnees after the Balkan Wars were concentrated in the Krajina districts of Bihać, Cazin, Ključ, Prijedor, Gradiška, and Prnjavor, while some *muhacirs* chose Anatolia or the Near East instead.[11] This allows the atlas to connect Ottoman frontier geography with twentieth-century population movement without treating “Krajina” as a static ethnic region.

## Source families to catalog

### Tahrir defterleri and rural geography

Use *tahrir* as a time-stamped administrative and fiscal survey, not as a modern census. The methodological literature distinguishes:

- *mufassal defter*: detailed settlements, male taxpayers, land, taxes, production, and local *kanunname*;
- *icmal defter*: summary revenue and *dirlik/timar* allocations;
- *evkaf/vakıf defter*: endowments and the revenues assigned to mosques, schools, bridges, waterworks, and similar institutions.

The *tahrir* framework records towns, villages, *mezraa* and other settlement forms, agricultural and livestock production, mines, industry, services, and some environmental clues. It is therefore the main bridge between urban and rural atlas layers.[12] Bosnian use must be critical: tax values are often estimated from prior production; units are not standardized; under-reporting and lumped revenues occur; and the registers cover taxable and administratively legible activity rather than every person or every economic practice. Cross-check *mufassal*, *icmal*, and *evkaf* against *kanunname*, sijil, cizye, mühimme, ruznamçe, and travel accounts.[12]

The TDV entry cites Bosnian register references including BA TD 18 and 24 and MAD 544 for a 1455 land case; the Turkish State Archives’ Bosnia-Herzegovina guide also points researchers to Tapu Tahrir Defterleri 56, 157, and 211.[3] These are repository leads, not a complete digitized corpus. Every atlas place extracted from a defter should store register number, folio, administrative hierarchy, original spelling, normalized form, and confidence of localization.

For rural coverage, begin with Bosna, Hersek, Zvornik, Krajina/Bihać, Neretva, Konjuh, Kladanj, Olovo, and the Sava–Drina corridors. Record *selo*, *mezraa*, *čiftlik*, pasture, mine, bridge, ferry, caravan route, and fortress as distinct feature types.

### Sidžil / şer‘iyye sicili

The Oriental Institute overview describes the kadi or Sharia court register as a regional archive. Besides court cases, it may contain *vakufname*, testaments, sales, debt instruments, orders, charters, and other official documents.[13] In Bosnia, the Gazi Husrev-beg collection is the most accessible major run: 88 Sarajevo sijils from 1552–1565 and 1725–1852.[4]

Do not imply equal preservation across cities. Sarajevo has the continuous series used by the conversion study; other kaza and nahiye runs are described as sparse.[5] Search for local registers under BHS *sidžil/sijil*, Turkish *şer‘iyye sicili*, and catalog terms such as *kadi defteri*, *mahkeme sicili*, and *protokol*. Note folio, court, kaza, date system, script, language, and whether a volume is original, copy, transcript, or translation.

### Salname

The Ottoman provincial yearbook is a bridge between administration and historical geography. The University of Chicago microform catalogue lists **Bosna 1291 [1874]**, fiche 3, catalogue code OFC221; access is through the Regenstein Library’s Middle East Department, not an open digital edition.[14] A Bosnian route study lists Bosna salnames from 1283 through 1295 as published sources.[10]

Salnames should be mined for administrative divisions, officials, population or confessional tables where present, schools, roads, bridges, mines, postal/telegraph infrastructure, and institutional names. Store both Hijri/Rumi and converted Gregorian dates, and preserve the printed Ottoman title. Treat each yearbook as an administrative snapshot; do not automatically merge its categories with the categories of tahrir or modern censuses.

### Vakıf / waqf and urban formation

The Gazi Husrev-beg archive fund holds approximately 1,500 endowment charters, including 421 originals, plus *vakıf* defters and related documents.[4] These are central for reconstructing mosque–madrasa–market–water–road complexes, property income, neighborhood boundaries, and the maintenance of public institutions. Ferhad Pasha’s Banja Luka waqf demonstrates that endowment records can map roads, water, bridges, inns, education, fortress, and credit alongside religious architecture.[9]

Index each vakıf as a network: founder; date; legal instrument; endowed property; revenue source; beneficiary institution; named neighborhoods and villages; administrators; repairs; and later ownership. Preserve *vakıfname* wording and distinguish original, certified copy, later transcript, translation, and modern scholarly edition.

### Modern and historical censuses

For the nineteenth century, do not combine Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian counts without a methodology field. Gelez identifies eight nineteenth-century enumerations: Ottoman 1851, 1865, 1870, and 1875; Austro-Hungarian 1879, 1885, 1895, and 1910.[11] A French study shows that earlier Ottoman fiscal counts did not enumerate the whole population; post-1850 operations were often male-based, locally administered, vulnerable to under-reporting, and affected by unrest and migration. The 1879 Austro-Hungarian count was also transitional and affected by recent emigration and conscription fears.[15]

The 1910 German publication, *Die Ergebnisse der Volkszählung in Bosnien und der Hercegovina vom 10. Oktober 1910*, was issued in Sarajevo in 1912 by the Landesdruckerei and includes an overview map of confessions. It is openly downloadable as a 782-page scan with OCR and PDF.[16] Use it for municipality and confession tables, but preserve the original German categories and note that they reflect Austro-Hungarian administrative concepts.

For the modern baseline, the Agency for Statistics’ 2013 census publication and GIS portal are authoritative starting points. The GIS offers interactive maps in Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, and English at Bosnia-wide, entity/district, canton, municipality, and settlement levels; thematic groups include migration, ethnicity, religion, mother tongue, education, agriculture, housing, and buildings. It also digitized and geographically aligned 1961, 1971, 1981, and 1991 census data to municipality boundaries.[17] Download the table/PDF and map products separately, because the current municipality or settlement boundary may not match an Ottoman kaza or a pre-1991 unit.

### Heritage

UNESCO’s Old Bridge Area of the Old City of Mostar is the clearest international heritage anchor for Ottoman urban form, reconstruction, and multicultural urban history.[6] The Commission to Preserve National Monuments defines a national monument broadly: movable and immovable property, architecture, archaeology, archives and collections, religious buildings, cemeteries, urban and rural sites, cultural landscapes, and related historic or natural places. It reports more than 800 designation decisions covering thousands of individual movable and immovable properties.[18]

Use the KONS catalogue as the national inventory authority, but retain the decision number, designation text, coordinates, protection zone, legal status, and current condition. The catalogue page is script- and interface-dependent, so city-level extraction should be checked manually and not inferred from a generic list. Heritage entries should distinguish Ottoman construction, Austro-Hungarian alteration, Yugoslav use, wartime destruction, and reconstruction.

### Migrations

Migration records must preserve both chronology and contested scale. Philippe Gelez treats Muslim emigration after the 1878 Austro-Hungarian occupation as complex: socioeconomic crisis and slowed natality mattered, while Austro-Hungarian policy also relied on Muslim inhabitants to stabilize the province; he therefore cautions against treating every departure as a single form of forced expulsion.[11] Krzysztof Popek summarizes estimates of an immediate 130,000-person exodus, later official counts of 32,625 emigrants in 1883–1905 with 4,042 returns and another 24,000 in 1906–1914, and scholarly estimates ranging from 100,000 to 300,000 for 1878–1914. Muslim population rose in absolute numbers from 449,000 in 1879 to 612,000 in 1910 while its share fell from 38.7% to 32.3%; these figures should be cited as debated historical estimates and not as a single definitive series.[19]

Track at least: 1875–78 wartime displacement; post-1878 emigration to Ottoman lands; 1882–84 and conscription-related departures; 1908 annexation; 1912–13 Balkan War movement and return; interwar and World War II changes; and 1992–95 displacement and return. Separate *muhacir*, refugee, emigrant, returnee, deportee, and internally displaced person where the source does so. Do not map “Muslim migration” as a synonym for “Bosniak migration” without a period-specific identity field.

## Maps and GIS stack

1. **Ottoman maps:** Elma Korić’s 2024 study examines an 1853 Ottoman map of the Sanjaks of Herzegovina and Scutari. It is written in Ottoman Turkish Arabic script and supplies evidence for mid-nineteenth-century Bosnian eyalet boundaries, administration, and mineral deposits. The author stresses that only one map from a larger corpus has yet been analyzed, so it is a high-value source sample, not a complete cartographic series.[20]
2. **Austro-Hungarian maps:** The Library of Congress has digitized the *Spezialkarte der Österreichisch-Ungarischen Monarchie*, begun around 1875 and issued in later editions, with manifests and a graphic index. Relevant sheets should be georeferenced for roads, settlements, administrative boundaries, military sites, and place-name variants.[21]
3. **Earlier cartography:** LOC identifies Waldseemüller’s 1516 *Carta marina*, a 1541 Ptolemaic map, Lipszky’s early-nineteenth-century map, and Schütz’s 1788 map as useful historical cartographic witnesses. These are not interchangeable with Ottoman administrative maps; record provenance and purpose.[21]
4. **Modern GIS:** Use BHAS 2013/1961–1991 thematic maps for official demographic and boundary baselines.[17]
5. **Current spatial base:** Geofabrik provides Bosnia-Herzegovina OSM PBF, Shapefile, GeoPackage, vector tiles, polygon extent, and update files. It is suitable for a current reference layer, not as evidence for historic settlement or route existence.[22]
6. **Historical route reconstruction:** The Konjuh study combines field archaeology, Austro-Hungarian maps, Ottoman salnames, and travel accounts. Its 8.29-kilometer Kladanj–Tuholj–Miljkovac segment and wider links to Zvornik, Olovo, Sarajevo, and Tuzla show how the atlas can connect urban nodes to mountain passes, villages, and markets.[10]

For every geospatial feature, store source map, sheet/page, original spelling, normalized name, date, geometry type, coordinate reference system, digitization method, and confidence. Use separate layers for historic polities, cadastral or survey units, settlements, route segments, hydrology, waqf properties, heritage, and modern boundaries. Do not dissolve historic boundaries into current cantons or entities.

## Archive and discovery leads

The Library of Congress guide points to the Virtual Library of Bosnia and Herzegovina (COBISS), the National and University Library catalogue, Virtual Library of Serbia, InfoBiro’s digitized newspapers, Archives Portal Europe, and other openly available resources.[23] The Gazi Husrev-beg Library reports about 200,000 library items in Arabic, Turkish, Persian, Bosnian, and other European languages; its research room provides digitized manuscript and archive material on-site.[24] UNESCO records 10,585 unique manuscript units and approximately 20,000 texts in the Gazi collection, which is catalogued, microfilmed, and digitized.[7]

Preservation loss is material evidence. The Oriental Institute article notes that the institute’s collections were destroyed by fire in Sarajevo in 1992, while the Gazi collection survived as a major regional manuscript resource.[13] Record destruction, war damage, dispersed copies, and later translations as part of the source history rather than treating absence as evidence of nonexistence.

## Priority build order for Rumelia Atlas

1. Build a gazetteer with Ottoman, German, BHS, Turkish, French, and current forms for Sarajevo, Mostar, Travnik, Banja Luka, Tuzla, Bihać, Zvornik, Jajce, Kladanj, Olovo, Cazin, Ključ, Prijedor, Gradiška, Neretva crossings, Konjuh routes, and major fortresses.
2. Add time-bounded administrative polygons from tahrir, salname, Austro-Hungarian, Yugoslav, and current sources, preserving uncertainty and competing reconstructions.
3. Index the Gazi Husrev-beg sijils and vakıf records first, then expand to Travnik’s vHMML manuscripts and local cantonal/state archives.
4. Link each settlement to tahrir *mufassal/icmal/evkaf* records, salname year, sijil volume/folio, vakıf charter, census table, heritage decision, and map sheet where available.
5. Publish demographic charts only with category labels unchanged. Put religion, national affiliation, mother tongue, and archival language in separate fields.
6. Add migration events as dated flows with origin, destination, group label, reason as stated by source, estimate range, and confidence level.

## Research gaps and cautions

- There is no single open, normalized digital corpus for all Bosnian tahrir defters. Register numbers found in guides and secondary works are discovery leads requiring archival verification.
- Sarajevo’s sijil run is unusually rich; other city and kaza registers are uneven, so absence of a comparable series for Mostar, Travnik, Tuzla, Banja Luka, or Bihać must not be mistaken for absence of court activity.
- The Bosna salname at Chicago is microfiche-access rather than a convenient open scan. Other years and copies may exist in Turkish, Bosnian, German, or local repositories.
- Nineteenth-century Ottoman counts, Austro-Hungarian censuses, Yugoslav censuses, and the 2013 census used different units, objectives, and identity categories. Avoid percentage time series until the unit and category transformations are documented.
- Migration totals after 1878 vary sharply by source and definition. Present ranges and explain legal/illegal crossings, returnees, and later reclassification.
- Heritage catalogues include legal and conservation categories that are not equivalent to “Ottoman monument.” Preserve the designation text and historical layers.
- The 1853 Ottoman map study and the Konjuh road study are strong entry points but do not constitute complete map or route corpora.
