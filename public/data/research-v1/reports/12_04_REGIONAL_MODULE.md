# Dobruja / Dobrogea / Dobrudzha — research memo for Rumelia Atlas

## Scope and framing

Dobruja is a single historical landscape now divided between southeastern Romania and northeastern Bulgaria. For an atlas, the safest unit is therefore a cross-border region with time-stamped political geographies, not a present-day Romanian or Bulgarian administrative unit. The region is also a problem of category translation: Ottoman records often privilege religion and legal status, later states prefer nationality, and community sources use overlapping labels such as Turk, Tatar, Nogai, Muslim, Crimean Tatar, and muhacir. These categories should not be collapsed into one layer.

The long Ottoman period is usually dated c. 1420–1878, although the area had older Turkic and Muslim presences and its political, demographic, and confessional boundaries varied over time.[1] [2] [3] The principal Romanian-side anchors are Constanța/Köstence, Tulcea/Tulça, Medgidia/Mecidiye (formerly Karasu), Babadag/Babadağ, and Mangalia/Mankalya. The main Bulgarian-side anchors are Silistra/Silistre, Dobrich/Hacıoğlu Pazarcık, and the northern Bulgarian/Dobrudzha villages extending toward Varna, Shabla, and the Danube.

For Rumelia Atlas, use separate layers for:

1. **Crimean Tatars** — including communities that later self-identify as Crimean Tatar, with an explicit migration-wave field.
2. **Nogais** — often intertwined with Crimean Tatar migration, but not identical; record origin and settlement separately where evidence allows.
3. **Turks** — Ottoman/Anatolian and later Turkish-speaking communities; do not treat every Muslim as Turkish.
4. **Muslims as a religious/legal category** — a historical umbrella that may include Turks, Tatars, Nogais, Circassians and other groups.
5. **Gagauz** — Turkic-speaking but predominantly Orthodox; keep outside a Muslim layer.
6. **Other communities** — Bulgarians, Romanians, Circassians, Germans, Jews, Greeks, Armenians, Russians/Lipovans and others, with dates and source confidence.
7. **Institutions and heritage** — mosques, cemeteries, dervish sites, waqfs, fortifications, schools, museums, cemeteries, newspapers and community organizations.

## Chronology: movement, war and state change

### Before the major nineteenth-century migrations

Muslim presence in the wider Romanian territory predates the Ottoman conquest; Pecheneg and Cuman movements are part of the longer background. In Dobruja, Ottoman rule consolidated a mixed military, commercial, agrarian and religious landscape. Mosques, medreses, tekkes/zaviyes, caravanserais, baths, bridges, fountains, waqfs, timar-holders and Danube fortifications are documented as material traces of the Ottoman centuries.[6] The region was strategically important because it linked the lower Danube, the Black Sea and routes toward Constantinople and Crimea.

The historical Muslim population should not be represented as a single late-arriving block. Turkish-speaking Muslims had an Ottoman/Anatolian component, while Crimean Tatar and Nogai arrivals came in multiple waves, including movement after Russian annexation of Crimea and after Russo-Ottoman wars. Some Tatar groups were already established in Dobruja before the nineteenth-century mass migrations.[1] [2]

### Russian annexation of Crimea and early migration waves

The first intensified migration sequence followed the Russian annexation of the Crimean Khanate in 1783. The Turkish-language migration survey of the Kırım Türkleri Kültür ve Yardımlaşma Derneği identifies major waves in 1792–93, 1802–03, 1812–13 and the 1830s, generally following Russo-Ottoman wars or heightened Russian pressure.[5] Earlier Nogai and Crimean Tatar movements from the northern Black Sea and Budjak/Bucak should be mapped separately from the much larger 1859–61 exodus.

The 1812 Russian annexation of Bessarabia and the wider reshaping of the northern Black Sea frontier also redirected Nogai and Tatar movement toward Ottoman Dobruja. A source-critical atlas should keep **origin**, **route**, and **final settlement** separate: not every migrant who reached Dobruja stayed there, and many later moved onward to Anatolia, Syria or other Ottoman provinces.

### Crimean War, 1853–56, and the 1859–61 mass migration

The largest nineteenth-century movement followed the Crimean War. The Turkish migration history records that roughly ten thousand Tatars were evacuated from Allied-occupied areas in 1856, followed by a much larger movement in the next decade.[5] Nogai movement began on a major scale in 1859; the same source describes the northern Nogai steppe as almost emptied through the 1859 migration and subsequent smaller waves. The 1860–61 Crimean Tatar movement is commonly described in the same source as at least 200,000 people leaving Crimea for Ottoman lands, although that is a total migration figure and must not be read as a Dobruja settlement count.[5]

Dobruja became both a destination and a transit zone. A number of Crimean Tatar and Nogai migrants were placed in Ottoman Rumelia, while others continued to Anatolia. Medgidia/Mecidiye is a particularly important settlement anchor: the Turkish association history describes it as built by order of Sultan Abdülmecid for Crimean Tatar migrants after the Crimean War.[5] This is a strong candidate for a settlement-history feature, but the exact founding and population figures should be cross-checked against Ottoman settlement registers and Romanian cadastral records before publication.

### Russo-Ottoman War, 1877–78, and the Berlin settlement

The 1877–78 war produced a second rupture. The Treaty of Berlin assigned the Danubian subprovince of Tulcea, approximately northern Dobruja, to Romania; the rest of the former Danube Province became the Principality of Bulgaria, while Eastern Rumelia became an autonomous Ottoman province until its annexation by Bulgaria in 1885.[3] During the war, more than half a million Muslims fled the Balkans, though most left for Ottoman Anatolia and Syria rather than remaining in Dobruja.[3]

Northern Dobruja's population count is source-sensitive. A table attributed to the French consulate in Tulcea gives **225,753 total** for northern Dobruja, including **126,924 Muslims**: 48,784 Turks, 71,146 Tatars and 6,994 Circassians; it lists 46,504 Romanians and 30,237 Bulgarians, with other Jews, Greeks, Armenians, Russians and Germans.[2] Another administrative synthesis cites approximately **169,000 inhabitants** for northern Dobruja in 1878.[12] Do not combine these figures. They likely reflect different boundaries, dates, or methods; use them as competing historical estimates with a methodology note.

Some Muslim residents of Dobruja fled during the war and later sought to return. Romania restricted reimmigration through certificates of origin, and Circassians were treated separately because of their association with wartime irregular forces. Crimean Tatar and Circassian emigrants protested land appropriation and reimmigration policy to Ottoman authorities.[3] This is a central atlas theme: post-1878 state-building changed not only political borders but also property, citizenship, settlement and the visibility of Muslim communities.

### Romanian integration, 1878–1913

Romania initially established three provisional counties in Dobruja in September 1878: Tulcea, Constanța and New Silistra. The early districts included Babadag, Tulcea, Măcin, Sulina, Constanța, Mangalia, Hârșova, Medgidia and Silistra Nouă. The county seat of New Silistra moved first to Cernavodă and then to Medgidia before April 1879.[12]

The 1880 reorganization created two counties, Tulcea and Constanța. Tulcea had the districts of Babadag, Tulcea, Măcin and Sulina; Constanța had Constanța, Mangalia, Medgidia, Hârșova and Silistra Nouă. The townships listed in the administrative synthesis include Tulcea, Chilia Veche, Babadag, Sulina, Isaccea, Măcin, Mahmudia, Constanța, Medgidia, Cernavodă, Hârșova, Mangalia, Cuzgun and Ostrov.[12]

Romanian integration was not simply an administrative renaming. Iordachi characterizes the 1878–1913 project as a combination of ethnic colonization, cultural homogenization, economic modernization and a separate citizenship regime.[11] The 1880 Law on the Organization of Dobruja and the 1882 immovable-property law converted Ottoman land relations into a new state-controlled property system; land title verification and redistribution contributed to the impoverishment and departure of some Turkish and Tatar cultivators.[2] [3] Between 1889 and 1914, 82,127 hectares were redistributed to Romanian colonists in one cited synthesis.[2]

### Twentieth-century border change and population exchange

Northern and Southern Dobruja changed state several times. Southern Dobruja was transferred from Bulgaria to Romania in 1913 and returned to Bulgaria in 1940 under the Treaty of Craiova. The 1940 population exchange moved approximately 62,000 Bulgarians from northern Dobruja southward, while Romanians, Aromanians and Megleno-Romanians moved north.[19] These movements must be modeled separately from the nineteenth-century Crimean Tatar/Nogai migrations and from later Turkish emigration.

## Community categories, kept separate

### Crimean Tatars

Crimean Tatars are a Turkic, Sunni Muslim community with a strong Crimea-centered historical identity. In both Romanian and Bulgarian Dobruja, post-1783 migration waves intensified after the Crimean War, with settlement in villages and towns. Bulgarian ethnographic research describes the present Bulgarian community as descendants of several fifteenth–nineteenth-century waves, especially after 1853–56, concentrated in Dobruja and northern Bulgaria; it estimates roughly 4,000 Bulgarian Crimean Tatars based on 2002–09 ethnological work.[13] The figure is a modern community estimate, not a nineteenth-century population count.

Important cultural markers include the Crimean Tatar language, Sunni Islam, distinctive family and ritual practices, food, clothing, folklore, dance and adobe house forms. Bulgarian ethnography emphasizes multilingualism in Bulgarian, Turkish and Crimean Tatar and the role of language as an ethnic boundary.[13] A Bulgarian folklore article points to a substantial scholarly bibliography, including Stoyan Antonov, Yelis Erolova, Maria Mihaylova-Mravkarova and specialist work by Mándoky, Mollova and Drimba.[14]

For Romania, identity should be tracked as both ethnic and political. Adriana Cupcea shows how post-Ottoman Turkish nationalism and Crimean Tatar national movements circulated through newspapers, schools and intellectual networks in Dobruja; Crimean and Turkish reference points could coexist, with Turkey functioning as a protective state and Crimea as the historical homeland.[1] [2]

### Nogais

Nogais are not interchangeable with Crimean Tatars, even where communities intermarried, used related Kipchak speech forms or were grouped together by Ottoman and later Romanian/Bulgarian authorities. The 1859 Nogai migration from the northern steppe preceded and helped trigger wider Crimean Tatar movement.[5] In a GIS record, use a `community_label` field that preserves the source wording, an `origin_region` field, and a `relationship_to_crimean_tatar_category` field such as `distinct`, `overlapping`, or `unclear`.

The best current atlas lead for Nogai concentration is the Mecidiye/Medgidia area, but this should be corroborated with Ottoman settlement lists, Romanian cadastral material and community histories before drawing a precise village polygon. Avoid projecting modern ethnolinguistic boundaries backward onto Ottoman-era records.

### Turks

“Turk” in Dobruja can designate Ottoman/Turkish-speaking Muslims, a post-Ottoman ethnic identity, or a state/administrative classification. Turkish and Tatar communities shared mosques, schools, legal institutions and a broader Muslim social field, but the two identities were not identical. Cupcea documents the shift from religious solidarity toward Turkish or Tatar ethnonational forms in the twentieth century.[1]

A 1878 northern-Dobruja estimate gives 48,784 Turks, separate from 71,146 Tatars and 6,994 Circassians.[2] A 1880 Constanța County breakdown cited by Szabadi gives 14,974 Turks and 22,584 Tatars out of 64,902 inhabitants.[12] These figures are useful for period layers but should be presented with their source, boundary and classification metadata.

### Muslims as a religious category

Muslim is a religious category, not an ethnic synonym. In 1878 northern Dobruja, the French-consulate table groups Turks, Tatars and Circassians as Muslims, then separates them in the same table.[2] Ottoman and post-Ottoman administration frequently recorded confessional affiliation, property or legal status rather than modern nationality. Muslim cemeteries, mosques, tekkes, waqfs and dervish graves can therefore be mapped even where ethnic attribution is uncertain.

The French CNRS-linked EUREL overview describes traditional Muslim presence in Dobruja during the almost five centuries of Ottoman rule and notes that Romanian Muslims are predominantly Sunni and concentrated in Dobruja.[18] It gives modern religious-association and mosque data, but its current statistics should not be back-projected to the nineteenth century.

### Gagauz

Gagauz must remain a separate category. They are Turkic-speaking but predominantly Orthodox, and their origins are contested. A scholarly overview notes that the first mentions connect ancestors of the Moldovan Gagauz with historical Dobruja in the thirteenth century; it also presents competing origin theories and stresses the absence of early written Gagauz sources.[10] Part of the population moved toward Bessarabia in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, with explanations ranging from Russo-Ottoman war displacement to settlement following the departure of Nogai Tatars.[10]

The atlas should not place Gagauz inside a “Turkish Muslim” layer. Use separate fields for language, confession, self-identification and historical location. The Gagauz case is also a useful warning against treating language, religion and ethnicity as identical.

### Circassians

Circassians were a major Muslim refugee category in the nineteenth-century Ottoman Balkans. In the 1878 northern-Dobruja table they are listed separately at 6,994.[2] Hamed-Troyansky shows that Circassian refugees were disproportionately recruited into Ottoman irregular forces in the 1876–78 crisis and that postwar authorities often treated them differently from other Muslim returnees.[3] Circassian settlement should therefore be a separate migration and settlement layer, even where later assimilation makes local traces difficult to identify.

### Bulgarians, Romanians, Germans and other communities

Bulgarian and Romanian settlement histories are essential to interpreting Muslim and Tatar displacement. The northern-Dobruja map compiled by Eliznik identifies Bulgarian clusters south of Babadag and around Shabla but warns that Ottoman records often record Christians without distinguishing Romanian from Bulgarian, while continued nineteenth-century immigration complicates retrospective mapping.[19]

The 1940 exchange created another major Bulgarian/Romanian layer. German settlers are documented from the 1840s in Romanian Dobruja, mostly arriving from the Russian Empire; they formed about three percent of the regional population before the 1940 resettlement and lived in a linguistically, ethnically and religiously plural environment.[9] The same project points to archival, church, press, eyewitness and visual materials in German, Romanian and American collections. Jews, Greeks and Armenians appear in the 1878 northern-Dobruja demographic table; Russians and other groups should be added where a source supplies a place and date.[2]

## Place dossiers and atlas anchors

### Constanța / Köstence / Küstendje

Constanța was the principal maritime and administrative center of northern Dobruja. It became the seat of Constanța County in the provisional 1878 Romanian arrangement and remained the county seat after the 1880 reorganization.[12] The city belongs in layers for Ottoman port infrastructure, Muslim institutions, Turkish/Tatar civic life, Romanian state-building, migration, and modern heritage.

MIRAS maps the Central Muslim Cemetery in Constanța and includes Ottoman objects, Turkish and Tatar clothing, family photographs, Islamic architecture, education, religious life and intangible heritage.[7] The community archive is especially useful for twentieth-century memory and visual documentation, but its photographs are not automatically open-license assets.

### Tulcea / Tulça

Tulcea was the capital of the Ottoman Sanjak of Tulcea in the nineteenth-century Danube Vilayet; the sanjak contained thirteen kazas according to the administrative synthesis.[12] The 1878 settlement assigned the Tulcea subprovince to Romania. The town and surrounding delta connect river, maritime, Muslim, Tatar, Turkish, Lipovan/Russian, Romanian, Bulgarian and commercial histories.

The Tulcea-based Peuce research institute provides a strong GIS and heritage lead. It combines historical, topographic and cadastral maps, documentary sources, satellite imagery, UAV photography, field archaeology and GIS. It maps Ottoman fortification remains and related sites around Tulcea, Isaccea, Babadag, Hârșova and Medgidia.[6]

### Medgidia / Mecidiye / Karasu

Medgidia is the strongest settlement anchor for post-Crimean-War Crimean Tatar and Nogai migration. Its earlier Turkish name was Karasu; Mecidiye commemorates Sultan Abdülmecid. The Kırım association history describes the town as established for Crimean Tatar migrants after the Crimean War.[5] In the 1878–80 Romanian administrative system, Medgidia belonged first to New Silistra County and then to Constanța County; it also became the seat of New Silistra County after earlier moves.[12]

The Peuce GIS study identifies three Ottoman forts in the territory of Medgidia.[6] MIRAS has a Muslim cemetery entry for Medgidia.[7] Recommended atlas treatment: a city-level settlement marker with a confidence note for the migration association, plus a separate fortification and cemetery layer.

### Babadag / Babadağ

Babadag was a major Ottoman military and religious center and later the seat of a Tulcea County district. The Romanian administrative source lists Babadag as a district and township after 1880.[12] Peuce research identifies six Ottoman tabiyas around Babadag and treats Babadag as one of the best-preserved clusters for studying Ottoman fortification landscapes.[6]

MIRAS maps the Babadag Muslim cemetery and the Sarî Saltuk Baba dervish graveyard.[7] Babadag also belongs in a waqf and Islamic architectural layer. Modern heritage claims should be checked against monument registers and site-management information before publication; community and tourism pages are useful leads but not sufficient for legal status.

### Mangalia / Mankalya

Mangalia was a major Black Sea settlement and an administrative district in the 1878–80 Romanian arrangement.[12] It should be modeled with maritime trade, Ottoman/Turkish-speaking Muslim community history, Crimean Tatar/Turkish migration, mosque and cemetery heritage, and later Romanian tourism/urbanization. The present research pass verified its administrative role but did not locate a sufficiently authoritative, open source for a complete Ottoman-to-modern settlement chronology or a full heritage inventory. Treat town-level community attribution as a lead requiring a dedicated Mangalia source pass.

### Silistra / Silistre

Silistra was an Ottoman administrative and military center and gave its name to the Silistra sanjak/administrative sphere. After 1878, the Romanian “New Silistra” county included Silistra Nouă and later became administratively connected to Medgidia and Constanța; the postwar border settlement also made Silistra a key point for the Romanian–Bulgarian divide.[12] The town should be mapped as a cross-border administrative anchor rather than simply as a Bulgarian city.

Silistra is also an important heritage and memory site for the southern side. Copernico documents a folklore group sign in Silistra, but explicitly marks that photograph “free access – no reuse”; do not treat it as an atlas asset without separate permission.[9] Bulgarian museum and archive material should be pursued for Ottoman, Turkish, Tatar, Bulgarian and 1913–40 layers.

### Adjacent areas

The minimum adjacent-area set is:

- **Isaccea** — Danube crossing, Ottoman fortress and dervish-graveyard context; Peuce maps five tabiyas in the area and MIRAS lists the Ishak Baba graveyard.[6] [7]
- **Măcin** — Danube/riverine fortress landscape and former Tulcea County district; Peuce documents Ottoman fortification remains.[6] [12]
- **Hârșova** — former Constanța County district and Ottoman Danube fortress zone; Peuce maps two tabiyas around Hârșova.[6] [12]
- **Sulina, Chilia Veche and Mahmudia** — delta, port, border and mixed-population contexts within the Tulcea administrative sphere.[12]
- **Cernavodă, Cuzgun and Ostrov** — southern and western routes linking Constanța, the Danube and New Silistra administrative histories.[12]
- **Dobrich and Shabla** — Bulgarian-side Dobrudzha settlement and ethnographic anchors; the old-village map identifies a Bulgarian cluster around Shabla and a cluster south of Babadag.[19]
- **Varna and northeastern Bulgaria** — secondary urban and community context for Bulgarian Crimean Tatars; the Bulgarian folklore bibliography includes work on Tatar speech in Varna.[14]

## Ottoman administration, names and GIS modeling

### Administrative sequence

A practical time-sliced administrative model is:

1. **Ottoman frontier and province, fifteenth–eighteenth centuries:** Dobruja as a border, military and settlement zone connected to the Silistra administrative sphere.
2. **Danube Vilayet, 1864–78:** the region was reorganized into Silistra and Tulcea sanjaks; the Tulcea sanjak contained thirteen kazas and was transferred to Romania in 1878.[12]
3. **Romanian provisional counties, 1878–80:** Tulcea, Constanța and New Silistra, with districts including Babadag, Mangalia, Medgidia, Hârșova and Silistra Nouă.[12]
4. **Romanian counties after 1880:** Tulcea and Constanța; New Silistra was absorbed into Constanța.[12]
5. **Bulgaria and Romania after 1878:** Southern Dobruja became part of Bulgaria, although its exact international status and later border revisions require date-specific mapping.
6. **1913–40 and after 1940:** Romania’s annexation of Southern Dobruja in 1913, its return to Bulgaria in 1940, and the associated population exchange.[19]

### Name handling

Store names as a multilingual, date-bounded alias table rather than replacing one name with another. Minimum fields should be `modern_name`, `romanian_name`, `bulgarian_name`, `ottoman_turkish_or_latinized_name`, `historical_spellings`, `administrative_unit`, `valid_from`, `valid_to`, `source`, and `confidence`. Commonly used atlas aliases include Constanța/Köstence or Küstendje, Medgidia/Mecidiye/Karasu, Babadag/Babadağ, Tulcea/Tulça, Mangalia/Mankalya and Silistra/Silistre. These spellings should be checked against the exact historical map or register used for each period, because transliteration and administrative usage vary.

### GIS layers and workflow

Recommended layers:

- **Political boundaries:** Ottoman sanjaks/kazas, Danube Vilayet, 1878 provisional counties, 1880 Romanian counties, 1913 and 1940 borders, and present-day counties/oblasti.
- **Settlement points and polygons:** city, town, village, muhacir settlement, abandoned or depopulated village, colony, and resettled village, each with date and source.
- **Community attributes:** source wording, self-identification, religion, language, origin, and whether the evidence is census, administrative, ethnographic, oral-history or later memory.
- **Migration routes:** Crimea, Nogai steppe, Budjak/Bessarabia, Ottoman Rumelia, Dobruja, Anatolia and Syria; separate direct and onward routes.
- **Heritage:** mosques, cemeteries, dervish graves, waqfs, schools, tekkes, forts/tabiyas, waterworks, cemeteries, museums and archival collections.
- **Map provenance:** scan URL, original cartographer, publication date, map date, georeferencing method, control points, distortion, license and attribution.
- **Uncertainty:** use explicit confidence values and a `source_type` field; never turn a conjectural village ethnicity into a definitive polygon.

Peuce provides a tested methodological model: digitize historical plans, overlay them on orthophotos or satellite images, inspect in the field, and integrate results into GIS. The study identifies five tabiyas at Isaccea, six at Babadag, five at Tulcea, two at Hârșova and three forts in Medgidia.[6] This is immediately reusable as a research pattern, but the article’s figures and datasets should not be copied into the atlas without checking publication rights.

## Heritage, archive and map sources

### High-value repositories and projects

- **MIRAS virtual museum of Turks and Tatars of Dobruja:** a Romanian government/Institute for Research on National Minorities project with more than 850 photographs and objects, an interactive map, Ottoman objects, clothing, photographs, postcards, family photographs, music, food, cemeteries and dervish sites. It has Romanian, Tatar and Turkish interfaces and directly covers Constanța, Medgidia, Babadag, Tulcea, Isaccea and Valu lui Traian.[7]
- **Regional History Museum Dobrich:** Bulgarian museum established in 1953 with collections from the sixth millennium BCE to the present, a historical archive, research activity and exhibitions on Dobruja.[8]
- **Peuce / Gavrilă Simion Eco-Museum Research Institute, Tulcea:** archaeological, cartographic and GIS research on Ottoman fortifications and lost sites.[6]
- **Copernico / IKGS Dobruja project:** German-language and transnational research using state and church records, reports, press, eyewitness accounts and visual sources in German, Romanian and American archives.[9]
- **Romanian National Archives:** use the local-archives portal to identify county branches and request Constanța/Tulcea material; the current pass confirms the portal but did not inspect individual fonds.[20]
- **Ottoman archival leads:** Ottoman population, land and settlement records should be sought through the Başbakanlık/Presidential Ottoman Archives and published studies of tahrir and population registers. Register terms and localities need Ottoman Turkish expertise; do not assume that a modern place-name search will retrieve the historical record.

### Maps

Wikimedia Commons has a useful category with more than forty Dobruja map files, including historical borders, topographic maps, ethnic/religious maps, a German marine map from 1917, the Reclus ethnological map and the 1878–79 map.[16] The file **Map of Dobrudja, 1878–1879.jpg** is a 1887 reproduction from Rudolf Bergener’s *Rumänien. Eine Darstellung des Landes und der Leute*, depicting the area between November 1878 and 9 April 1879; the Commons page marks it public domain.[15]

Other map candidates include the historical-map category, ethnic/religious maps for Southern Dobruja, and French/German-language topographic maps. Each file must be checked individually. The Commons category page states that files carry their own licenses; the CC BY-SA notice for Commons text does not automatically license every image in the category.[16]

OpenStreetMap is suitable for modern base data and is identified by Copernico as ODbL-licensed.[9] Historical overlays require separate provenance and should not be presented as OSM data merely because they are aligned to an OSM base.

## Licensing and rights flags

- **MIRAS / Romanian government:** the MIRAS site links to Romanian government legal conditions. The Secretariat General of Government says reproduction is authorized with source attribution within copyright law, but a prior agreement is required where the material itself indicates one; individual photographs, personal data and community-contributed materials require item-level checking.[7] [17]
- **Regional History Museum Dobrich:** the website states that published photographs and texts belong to the museum and cannot be used or copied without explicit written permission. Treat museum images, scans and exhibition text as **permission required**, not open data.[8]
- **Copernico images:** at least one Silistra image is explicitly “free access – no reuse.” Do not reuse it in Rumelia Atlas without permission.[9]
- **Wikimedia Commons:** the 1887 Bergener map is marked public domain on its file page; other Commons maps may be CC BY, CC BY-SA, public domain or subject to other terms. Capture the exact file-page license and attribution in the atlas asset record.[15] [16]
- **OpenStreetMap:** use under ODbL with attribution and share-alike obligations for database-derived material; treat third-party historical layers separately.[9]
- **EUREL:** the French CNRS-linked page displays CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. It is suitable for citation and noncommercial reference, but not for adapting or republishing text without checking the license.[18]
- **Scholarly PDFs and journal figures:** access to a PDF does not equal permission to reproduce figures, maps or photographs. Cite the article, link to the publisher, and request permission for republication of any figure. This applies to Brill, Peuce, the Bulgarian folklore journal and the Romanian working paper.[4] [6] [14]

## Source assessment and gaps

The strongest verified evidence in this pass is the combination of the Romanian ISPMN demographic/identity study, Iordachi’s French/English integration study, Hamed-Troyansky’s recent post-Ottoman land analysis, Szabadi’s administrative synthesis, Peuce’s GIS/fortification article, MIRAS, the Bulgarian ethnography portal, the Turkish migration-history page, and the Copernico project.[1]–[14]

The most important unresolved issues are:

1. **Exact Ottoman settlement lists:** identify and transcribe tahrir, temettuat, population and land registers for Silistra/Tulcea/Babadag/Köstence/Mecidiye and their villages.
2. **Nogai versus Crimean Tatar village attribution:** community labels overlap; village-level claims need Ottoman or community-source corroboration.
3. **Bulgarian-side quantitative data:** obtain the Dobrich museum’s Ottoman and post-1878 registers, ethnographic fieldwork and map metadata, especially for Silistra, Dobrich, Shabla and villages around the southern border.
4. **Gagauz spatial precision:** the historical-Dobruja connection is well attested in the overview, but town- and village-level location data require specialist scholarship and primary sources.
5. **Mangalia heritage and settlement history:** verify mosque, cemetery, waqf and migration claims with Romanian monument records and local museum sources.
6. **Population-count reconciliation:** document why the 169,000 and 225,753 northern-Dobruja totals differ before building a comparative demographic chart.
7. **French and German primary materials:** the French consular table, French ethnographic works and German travel/map sources should be located in full scans, catalogued and, where possible, georeferenced.
8. **Rights clearance:** request written permissions for museum photographs, MIRAS item-level assets, Peuce figures and any community-supplied family photographs. Use public-domain Commons maps and self-created GIS layers wherever possible.
