# Romania / Dobruja — Rumelia Atlas research memo (v1)

Romanian Dobruja (Dobrogea), especially the present counties of Constanța and Tulcea, is production-ready as a **layered historical region**, not as a single timeless ethnic territory. The evidence supports a map stack that separates Ottoman administrative units, settlement and migration episodes, self-identified ethnicity, mother tongue, religion, and surviving/remembered heritage. The central caution is that “Turk,” “Tatar,” “Nogai,” “Gagauz,” “Muslim,” and “Turkish-speaking” are not interchangeable categories.

## 1. What can be stated securely

Dobruja was an Ottoman borderland from the early fifteenth century until the Russo-Ottoman War and the 1878 settlement. The late-Ottoman administrative frame changed repeatedly: the region was linked to the sancak of Silistria in the Rumelia system; northern zones were organized at different times as the kazas of Hârșova, Isaccea, Hârșova-Babadag and Kara-su/Medgidia; and the 1864 Tanzimat reorganization placed it in the Danube (Tuna) vilayet. The nomenclature is chronological, not contradictory: “Dobruja,” “Dobruca,” “Dobrudja” and “Dobrogea” refer to a changing historical region, while sancak, kaza, vilayet, nahiye and nahiye/village units describe different administrative levels. The strongest concise syntheses are Popescu’s account of Ottoman Dobrudja, the Greek Black Sea Encyclopedia’s administrative history, and the Turkish TDV entry.[1] [2] [3]

At the 1878 transfer, Northern Dobruja entered Romania; the Russian occupation and Romanian takeover were followed by a special, exceptional regime. Romanian provisional counties created in September–November 1878 were Tulcea, Constanța/Kustendge and New Silistra, with Medgidia becoming the seat of New Silistra before the 1880 reorganization. The 1880 law consolidated the province into Tulcea and Constanța counties, with districts and townships including Babadag, Tulcea, Isaccea, Constanța and Medgidia. Residents acquired citizenship in 1878 but enjoyed only a local form of citizenship and limited political rights until gradual emancipation in 1908–1913. This is a key interpretive layer for any “Romanian census” map: the state was not simply counting a settled nation in a neutral administrative frame; it was also reorganizing, taxing, settling and assimilating a newly acquired border province.[4] [5]

Migration is multi-directional and cumulative. Early Seljuk/Turkic settlement traditions associate the thirteenth-century arrival of Izzeddin Keykavuz and Sarı Saltuk with Dobruja, but this belongs to the pre-Ottoman memory/history layer and should not be merged with later Ottoman Turkish colonization. Ottoman settlement and military organization brought Muslim populations into a strategic serhad landscape. Nogai and other Tatar movements from the northern Black Sea and Bucak intensified in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; Crimean Tatar migration accelerated after the 1853–1856 Crimean War. The Ottoman/Turkish and Crimean Tatar/Nogai histories overlap in settlement, religion and political affiliation, but they are not one ethnic genealogy. The modern Turkish and Tatar communities also experienced emigration to the Ottoman Empire/Turkey after 1878, again after 1923, and in later treaty and family-reunification waves. Cupcea’s study is especially useful because it shows how religious belonging could precede or coexist with ethnic self-definition, and how the post-1878 “hijra” memory framed Turkey as *ak toprak* and *devlet baba*.[6] [7]

## 2. Separate the four analytical layers

**Community / ethnicity.** For atlas purposes, use “Turks,” “Tatars,” “Nogais,” “Gagauz,” “Romanians,” “Bulgarians,” “Lipovans,” “Roma,” “Greeks,” “Jews,” “Armenians,” Germans and other labels only when a source uses that label or when a census category is explicit. A community may be a self-identification, a state classification, a local exonym, or a later historiographical reconstruction. In particular, “Tatar” in Dobruja includes Crimean Tatar and Nogai histories, and community narratives may preserve distinctions (“a Tatar is one thing, a Nogai another”) that are not visible in a census table.[7] [8]

**Language.** Mother tongue is a separate variable. Turkish in Romania is an Oghuz/Turkic language associated with the Turkish minority; Dobrujan Tatar varieties are generally treated as Crimean Tatar/Kipchak-related, with Nogai histories and local multilingualism; Gagauz is an Oghuz Turkic language. Language shift to Romanian, and bilingual or mixed households, make ethnic label and mother tongue diverge. The 1930 Romanian census is unusually valuable because it collected ethnicity, mother tongue and religion concurrently. The 2011 census confirms the need for separation: among people declaring Turkish ethnicity, 85.6% reported Turkish as mother tongue and the remainder Romanian; Turkish, Tatar and Russian each formed roughly one person in a thousand among declared mother tongues nationally, while the census did not equate ethnicity with language.[9] [10]

**Religion.** The historic Turkish and Tatar communities of Dobruja are predominantly Sunni Muslim, but “Muslim” is not a synonym for either ethnic community. Ottoman administration often organized social life through religion-based millets; Cupcea’s research documents the transition from a primarily religious self-understanding toward ethnic community organization in the twentieth century. Conversely, Gagauz are Turkic-speaking Orthodox Christians, not Muslim Turks or Tatars. The Gagauz evidence must therefore be mapped as a distinct community-language-religion combination: Gagauz / Oghuz Turkic / Orthodox. Historical documents often classified Gagauz as Bulgarians or other Christian groups, and the Romanian evidence base is thin; do not infer Gagauz from “Turkish” or from Muslim data.[6] [11] [12]

**Citizenship and political status.** Ottoman subjecthood, Romanian citizenship, local citizenship, residence, ethnicity and religion are different fields. The 1878–1913 special regime and later assimilation policies affected landholding, public employment, schooling and representation. A production schema should therefore include `status_type`, `source_date`, `declared_or_assigned`, and `administrative_unit`, rather than use “population” as an unqualified count.[4] [5]

The place-name layer should preserve both historical and current strings. Romanian county and municipality names are the display defaults, with Ottoman/Turkish variants in a multilingual alias table: Constanța—Köstence/Kustendge; Tulcea—Tulça; Babadag—Babadağı; Medgidia—Karasu/Kara-su and Mecidiye; Isaccea—İsakça/Isakçe. Locality-name research finds more than one hundred Romanian/Turkish-Tatar doublets or inherited toponyms in Constanța County; names are evidence of historical contact, not automatic evidence of a surviving ethnic majority.[21]

## 4. Censuses and demographic data pipeline

The official Romanian census history lists the 1859–1860, 1899, 1912, 1930, 1941, 1948, 1956, 1966, 1977, 1992, 2002 and 2011 censuses. The 1859–1860 enumeration covered the two principalities and recorded population by nationality and religious cult, but it predates Romanian sovereignty over Dobruja. The 1899 and 1912 enumerations are useful for the Romanian-ruled territory including Dobruja. The 1930 census is the key interwar source: it recorded ethnicity, mother tongue and religion simultaneously. The official census-history page and INSSE historical-series volume should be the authority for census dates, territorial coverage and publication provenance.[9] [22]

Territorial comparability is a major issue. INSSE warns that historical series change territorial coverage: before 1878 Dobruja is not included in the same way as later Romania; 1880–1912 excludes the southern counties of Caliacra and Durostor; 1919–1939 covers the Kingdom of Romania; and later series use different historical or reconstructed territories. The German University of Oldenburg table is useful as a working series for Northern Dobruja, but its totals and boundaries must be stored with the source definition rather than merged with “Romanian Dobruja” or the 1930 Romanian sector including the Cadrilater.[9] [23]

Useful benchmark points are:

- **1877–1878 Russian statistics:** incomplete, excluding Mangalia, Cernavodă and Silistra in the cited series; one tabulation gives 79,357 inhabitants in included districts, so it should not be used as a full-province total.[24]
- **1878–1880 transition:** approximately 169,000 inhabitants for Northern Dobruja appears in the public-administration literature, while other 1878 sources give totals of 80,000–100,000 because they count different areas, families, or incomplete districts. Preserve each denominator and geography.[4] [5] [24]
- **1880 Constanța:** 64,902 in the early count cited above, with Turks and Tatars together forming a very large share.[4]
- **1930:** the Romanian sector including the Cadrilater is reported at 815,475 in one census-based analysis; the same study warns that 1930 data and territorial framing are not identical to Northern Dobruja. County-level Turkish/Tatar counts should be taken from the original *Recensământul general al populației României* tables, not inferred from secondary percentages.[23]
- **2011:** the official results count 27.7 thousand Turks and 20.3 thousand Tatars in Romania; around 90% of declared Turks were in Constanța, Tulcea and Bucharest, while 96.6% of declared Tatars were in Constanța. These are self-declared ethnicity results, with non-response and administrative-source cases excluded from the characteristic-specific denominators.[10]

The atlas should store census rows as `census_year`, `publication_year`, `territorial_definition`, `unit_type`, `unit_name_source`, `ethnicity_declared`, `mother_tongue_declared`, `religion_declared`, `count`, `denominator`, `enumeration_method`, and `notes`. Keep “unknown/not declared” rather than redistributing it. For 1930, digitize the three variables as separate tables linked by geography, then join only for analytical views.

## 5. Migration and identity sequence for map layers

1. **Pre-Ottoman Turkic and Christian-Turkic memory (thirteenth–fourteenth centuries).** Sarı Saltuk and Seljuk/Golden Horde narratives belong in a documented “origin traditions / early settlement” layer with source confidence flags. Avoid projecting twentieth-century ethnicity backward onto medieval labels.[11] [12]
2. **Ottoman conquest, colonization and frontier organization (fifteenth–eighteenth centuries).** Map sancak/kaza change, military roads, forts, vakıf/türbe/mosque sites and Turkish toponyms. Babadag, Isaccea, Tulcea, Karasu and the Danube crossings are especially important.[1] [2] [3]
3. **Russian-Ottoman frontier and northern Black Sea migrations (late eighteenth–1856).** Map Bucak/Nogai and Crimean movements, as well as Christian Gagauz/Bulgarian movement in the opposite direction. Treat “exchange” narratives carefully: the scale, agency and timing vary by source.[2] [12] [24]
4. **Crimean War and Medgidia settlement (1853–1860s).** Map refugee settlement, land allocation, planned town formation and the Muslim seminary/institutional network.[3] [18]
5. **Tuna vilayet and 1878 Romanian takeover (1864–1880).** Map the Ottoman Danube vilayet, Russian occupation statistics, the three Romanian provisional counties, and the 1880 county/district reorganization.[2] [4] [5]
6. **Romanian settlement, land reform and out-migration (1880–1940).** Add Romanian colonization, Bulgarian population movement, Turkish/Tatar emigration to Turkey, German settlement/resettlement, and the 1913–1940 Cadrilater period. The German-language scholarship is particularly useful for transnational and micro-historical method, but its German-settler focus should not be mistaken for a complete Dobruja migration history.[23] [25]
7. **Communist and post-1990 urbanization.** Map collectivization, industrial/port/canal labor migration, rural depopulation, internal migration to Constanța/Medgidia/Tulcea and emigration. The Lanurile cemetery study gives a concrete microhistory: Ottoman-era villages Osman-Facı and Ebeköyü were renamed Bărăganu and Lanurile after 1878, and many residents later moved to Basarabi/Medgidia/Constanța or Turkey.[8]

## 6. Heritage inventory and GIS production

MIRAS, a Romanian Department for Interethnic Relations / ISPMN virtual museum, is the best immediately usable community-facing heritage source. It documents mosques, cemeteries, Ottoman objects, traditional clothing, photographs, family archives, food, music and oral-history materials. Its interactive map exposes named nodes in Constanța, Medgidia, Babadag, Tulcea and Isaccea, with coordinates and photo/virtual-tour assets. MIRAS is highly suitable for a first heritage layer, but its entries are not a substitute for the official national List of Historical Monuments, conservation reports, cadastral parcels or archival dating.[13] [16] [17] [19]

The Lanurile Muslim cemetery study is valuable because it expands the heritage definition beyond elite monuments. Ottoman documents identify Lanurile and Bărăganu as Ebeköyü and Osman-Facı in the kaza of Mangalia, inhabited by Turks and Tatars; the cemetery’s surviving stones record genealogy, memory and depopulation. This is a model for a Rumelia Atlas “community memory / rural material heritage” layer that includes cemeteries, wells, former mosques, abandoned villages and renamed localities rather than only standing monuments.[8]

A serious GIS workflow is already foreshadowed by Stănică’s 2016-onward project, published in the 2023 Turkish Art Congress proceedings. The project uses GIS/QGIS, GPS, aerial/drone photography, historical maps and satellite imagery to record Ottoman-period urban and rural sites, cemeteries, wells, water systems, fortifications and religious architecture. The authors explicitly note that Ottoman documents are sparse or inconsistent, traveller descriptions disagree, and fortress plans are often missing; the project identified more than fifty new cartographic sources. These caveats should become atlas confidence fields, not be hidden in the visualization.[20]

Recommended spatial layers:

- present administrative boundaries (Romanian county, city, commune and village), with source date;
- historical territorial extents: Ottoman sancak/kaza/vilayet, Russian occupation districts, Romanian 1878 provisional counties, 1880 counties/districts, 1913–1940 Cadrilater, and 1940–present border;
- settlement points with historical aliases and normalized coordinates;
- migration origin/destination edges with date range, group label and source confidence;
- census polygons or points with denominator and territorial definition;
- mosques, türbes, tekkes, medreses/seminary, cemeteries, wells, forts, ports, railway and road corridors;
- oral-history, family-photography and intangible-heritage records linked to a community and language/religion field;
- a source and rights table for every geometry, scan, transcription, translation and photograph.

For historical maps, the BCU Cluj digital library offers a 1913 Romanian map by Vasile T. Niculescu at 1:432,000, revised after the 1913 Bucharest treaty and based on Romanian General Staff and recent German maps. It is a good georeferencing base for the late Ottoman/post-Ottoman transition, but it is a Romanian national-era map with a political purpose and should not be treated as an Ottoman administrative map.[26] Archive.org provides ten scanned Ottoman **Salname-i Vilayet-i Tuna** volumes, Rumi years 1285–1294, with image PDFs and OCR; these are the best primary-source candidates for late Ottoman administrative, institutional and demographic extraction, but Ottoman-Turkish reading and OCR correction are required.[27] The Library of Congress also provides a digitized “Map Empire Ottoman: division administrative”; inspect its item metadata and high-resolution image before using it for Dobruja boundaries.[28]

Use present-day open boundaries and OSM only as reference basemaps, never as evidence of historical borders. Georeference each map with control points and record transformation, residual error, datum, scan source, and whether the map’s place labels have been transliterated, translated or normalized.

## 7. Production readiness

**Overall: medium-high for a cited regional narrative and a heritage/administrative prototype; medium for quantitative historical choropleths; low-to-medium for fine-grained ethnic or language polygons without original tables and archival checking.**

Ready now:

- a clearly bounded Northern Dobruja study area using present Constanța and Tulcea counties, with optional historical South Dobruja/Cadrilater extension;
- a dated Ottoman-to-Romanian administrative timeline;
- named place nodes and a robust heritage shortlist for Constanța, Tulcea, Babadag, Medgidia and Isaccea;
- a source-backed migration narrative for Crimean Tatars, Nogais, Turks, Romanians, Bulgarians, Germans, Lipovans and Gagauz-related movements;
- a census schema that separates ethnicity, mother tongue, religion, citizenship/status and territory;
- a first GIS production plan using MIRAS, BCU Cluj, Ottoman salnames and the Stănică GIS methodology.

Not ready without additional work:

- a single “Turkish/Tatar homeland” polygon;
- a map that treats Muslims as Turks or Tatars, or Gagauz as Muslim Turks;
- a direct comparison of 1878, 1880, 1899, 1930 and 2011 totals without territory/denominator harmonization;
- precise village-level ethnicity or language maps based only on later secondary tables;
- a definitive chronology or geometry for every mosque, fort, vakıf, türbe or cemetery;
- a claim that modern self-identification reproduces Ottoman millet categories.

## 8. Priority gaps and next research passes

1. **Obtain and transcribe original Romanian census volumes**, especially 1899, 1912 and 1930, at county, commune and locality level. The current evidence is strong for method and selected totals but incomplete for all six target places.
2. **Build a territorial crosswalk** between Ottoman kazas/sancak, Russian occupation districts, 1878 provisional counties, 1880 districts, 1913–1940 counties and current UATs. This is essential before any time-series map.
3. **Extract the Tuna vilayet salnames** in Ottoman Turkish, starting with Tulça/Tulcea, Babadağı, İsakça, Kara-su and Köstence entries. OCR is not reliable enough for names or numbers without human review.
4. **Verify monument status and construction dates** in the official Romanian List of Historical Monuments and county heritage inventories; MIRAS currently gives excellent location/photo coverage but sparse chronological metadata on the pages fetched.
5. **Add Gagauz-specific Romanian evidence.** Locate the 1930 volume’s Gagauz/Bulgarian classifications, local lists for Beidaud, Stejaru, Agighiol and Izvoarele, church and school records, and oral histories. Do not infer Gagauz settlement from generic “Turkic” or “Bulgarian” rows.
6. **Separate Crimean Tatar, Nogai and Turkish migration episodes.** The literature and community memory use overlapping labels; capture each claim with origin, date, settlement, source language and confidence.
7. **Digitize cemeteries and renamed villages** as community microhistory evidence. Lanurile/Bărăganu is a reproducible pilot; extend to rural sites around Medgidia, Babadag, Tulcea and Isaccea.
8. **Add language evidence beyond census labels:** school registers, newspapers, mosque/medrese records, family interviews, Tatar and Turkish publications, and Gagauz oral/linguistic materials.
9. **Rights and preservation review:** MIRAS imagery, BCU Cluj scans, Archive.org salnames and map images have different reuse terms; store rights, citation and derivative-work status per asset.
10. **Cross-language editorial review:** Romanian, Ottoman Turkish/Turkish, German and French place-name forms should be checked by native or specialist reviewers before public release. French coverage is presently the weakest part of this pass; Gallica/BnF and nineteenth-century French travel/administrative literature should be searched in the next round.
