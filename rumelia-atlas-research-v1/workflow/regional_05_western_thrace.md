# Western Thrace: regional readiness memo for Rumelia Atlas

**Region:** Western Thrace / Greek Thrace, today the Greek regional units of Xanthi, Rodopi and Evros. **Priority places:** Komotini/Gümülcine, Xanthi/İskeçe, Alexandroupoli/Dedeağaç, Genisea/Yenice and selected Rhodope mountain and rural settlements. **Readiness:** medium-high for administrative and heritage layers; medium for historical demography; medium-low for current ethnolinguistic mapping unless the Atlas represents identity as a contested, source-specific layer rather than a single population map.

Western Thrace is ready for a layered atlas, not for a single ethnic map. The region has unusually good source depth: Ottoman tax and population registers, late Ottoman provincial records, French-administered 1919–20 village and district statistics, Greek refugee and census records, municipal heritage inventories, and modern GIS-compatible gazetteers. The principal limitation is conceptual. The legal category preserved by Lausanne is a **Muslim minority**, while Turkish, Pomak, Muslim Roma/Romani, Bulgarian-speaking, Turkish-speaking, and other labels describe overlapping and sometimes situational identities. The Atlas should therefore keep administrative, linguistic, religious, self-identification and external classification as separate fields.

## 1. Regional frame and modern administrative baseline

For current mapping, Western Thrace should be represented by three regional units: Xanthi, Rodopi and Evros. The 2021 Hellenic Statistical Authority (ELSTAT) results give resident populations of 108,195 for Xanthi, 104,262 for Rodopi and 133,802 for Evros. The figures are for whole regional units, not for the Muslim minority. The same official table gives the principal municipalities as follows: Xanthi 66,875, Avdira 17,610, Myki 14,237 and Topeiros 9,473; Komotini 65,243, Arriana 14,905, Iasmos 12,247 and Maroneia–Sapes 11,867; Alexandroupoli 71,751, Didymoteicho 16,060, Orestiada 31,686, Samothraki 2,596 and Soufli 11,709.[1]

These numbers should be used as the current administrative denominator, with a clear note that municipality totals include many settlements and do not measure any ethnic or religious population. ELSTAT’s published 2021 tables provide sex, age, citizenship, place of birth, marital status and household variables. The published results page does not list ethnicity, religion or language tables. The 2021 census is therefore useful for settlement scale, age structure, citizenship and migration proxies, but not for a direct Turkish/Pomak/Roma count.[1] Historical maps should not be forced into the current municipality frame: the Ottoman kaza, sanjak and district boundaries, the French Inter-Allied districts, the interwar prefectures and the post-2011 Kallikratis municipalities are different geometries.

Use the following name pairs as controlled historical variants: **Komotini—Gümülcine**, **Xanthi—İskeçe**, **Alexandroupoli—Dedeağaç/Dedeagats**, **Genisea—Yenice-i Karasu/Yenice**, **Myki—Mustafçova**. Every variant needs a language, date range and source field. A Greek-language study notes that Ottoman-derived Turkish place names were removed from official Greek documentation after 1977, while they continued in everyday use among many Turkish speakers.[2] This makes historical names valuable evidence, but not proof of present-day identity.

## 2. Ottoman and modern administrative records

### Komotini/Gümülcine

Gümülcine entered Ottoman political and demographic history early. Turan Gökçe places its capture by Umur Bey in 1344 and its later incorporation by Gazi Evrenos Bey; in the Ottoman administrative system it became a kaza attached to the Paşa livası of Rumeli. Gökçe’s study uses tahrir, avarız, cizye and related registers, together with Ottoman archival, cadastral and Edirne provincial salname material. It records 14 neighbourhoods and about 2,836 people in 1454, rising to a late nineteenth-century urban population of 12,393.[3]

The Greek Ministry of Culture’s Egnatia heritage inventory adds a spatially useful urban account. The Ottoman settlement developed outside the Byzantine castle, while the castle housed Christian residents and, from the sixteenth century, Jewish residents. By around 1900 the city had 2,110 households: 1,450 Muslim, 500 Orthodox, 100 Jewish and 60 Armenian. This is a household count rather than an ethnic census and should be represented as such.[4]

Gümülcine became a sanjak capital in 1867. Tobacco, livestock, grain, silk and other commerce supported the late Ottoman urban expansion. The source base is strong enough to build a neighbourhood-level historical layer, but the Atlas should preserve the distinction between a household register, a religious affiliation, a neighbourhood label and an ethnic identity.

### Xanthi/İskeçe

İskeçe entered Ottoman territory in 1373 and was initially a village in the Yenice-i Karasu township of the Paşa sanjak, Rumeli province. Çakır and Civelek identify it as a vakıf village associated with the Bayezid II külliye in Edirne. Their study is based on Ottoman archival series including tahrir, Maliyeden Müdevver, Kâmil Kepeci and mühimme registers. They trace the transformation from a long-standing village into a Balkan city and emphasise the shift toward a commercially important Turkish city in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, even though the earlier settlement was largely Christian.[5]

The official Egnatia inventory supplies the urban chronology. Evliya Çelebi described a seventeenth-century Xanthi of about 500 houses, approximately half Christian and half Muslim, with Ottoman judicial and administrative officials. Tobacco was already famous by 1715; from the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries it became the dominant agricultural and commercial sector. After Genisea’s 1870 fire, Xanthi became the seat of the kaza in 1872, and the Thessaloniki–Constantinople railway reached the city in 1891.[6]

The Ottoman record trail for Xanthi is particularly useful for linking settlement, economy and infrastructure. The Atlas should connect the kaza, tobacco-growing hinterland, railway, khans, warehouses, mosques and refugee-era land redistribution instead of treating the city as an isolated monument cluster.

### Alexandroupoli/Dedeağaç

Dedeağaç is a different type of Ottoman place. It was a nineteenth-century port-and-railway town rather than an early Ottoman inland centre. The Aristotle University thesis by Maria Temirtsidi describes the town’s formation around 1860 under the Tanzimat, with the railway and port as the principal drivers of rapid urban growth. It cross-checks Greek sources against catalogues of the Ottoman Prime Minister’s Archive and identifies unused military, economic and political documentation as a major research opportunity.[7]

The Alexandroupoli municipal history records a small fishing settlement that grew after the Russo-Turkish War and expanded rapidly after the railway was built. During the Balkan Wars the town passed from Ottoman to Bulgarian control; after the Treaty of Neuilly and an Inter-Allied administration, it was incorporated into Greece on 14 May 1920. After the 1922 catastrophe and population exchange, refugees from Northern Thrace/Eastern Rumelia, Eastern Thrace and Asia Minor arrived. The town was again under Bulgarian occupation from 1941 to 1944.[8]

Dedeağaç should therefore be modelled as a late Ottoman mobility, transport and port node. It is not safe to infer that its modern municipal population represents the historic town or that the town’s Muslim share can be derived from current census data. Ottoman consular correspondence, railway and port records, 1919–20 French statistics, refugee settlement registers and municipal building records should be joined only after their spatial units are identified.

### Primary and institutional record leads

The Turkish State Archives publication *Osmanlı Belgelerinde Batı Trakya* is a major primary-source lead. Its catalogue includes documents on Gümülcine neighbourhoods and communities, including a Bulgarian neighbourhood named “Hürriyet,” an Armenian neighbourhood and Dedeağaç consular correspondence.[9] The very large PDF is difficult to mine automatically and needs page-level OCR and metadata extraction before individual records are cited.

The Bulgarian-language Sofia University dissertation abstract is another important archive lead. It reports work in the Greek archives of Komotini and Kavala, supplemented by the Bulgarian Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Central State Archives in Sofia. It treats the Pomak question through Greek state records, Bulgarian diplomatic material and the history of minority policy from 1945 to 1974.[10] This is a strong case for a bilingual archive plan rather than a Turkey-only Ottoman collection.

## 3. Muslim minority categories: keep the layers separate

### The legal category: Muslims of Western Thrace

The 30 January 1923 Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations made religion the operative legal criterion. Article 1 ordered the compulsory exchange of Turkish nationals of Greek Orthodox religion in Turkey and Greek nationals of Muslim religion in Greece. Article 2 excluded “the Moslem inhabitants of Western Thrace” from that exchange, alongside the Greek inhabitants of Constantinople.[11] The convention does not define a Turkish, Pomak or Roma ethnic category. It also contains property, community-foundation and Mixed Commission provisions that matter for vakıf and property mapping.

The legal category should appear in the Atlas as a treaty and state-administrative layer. It should not be converted into a homogeneous ethnic polygon. The later Greek official position, the Turkish official position and community self-identification are different source classes and should be labelled separately. The Turkish Foreign Ministry currently describes the population as a Turkish minority of about 150,000 and frames the main issue as denial of ethnic identity; this is a state position and should be cited as such, not used as a neutral demographic estimate.[12]

### Turkish-speaking and “Turkish-origin” Muslims

“Turkish-speaking” is a linguistic description, while “Turkish” or “Turkish-origin” may be an ethnic, national or political self-description. They should not be merged. A French-language study of the twentieth-century minority reports that the 1928 Greek census recorded about 85,000 Muslims as Turkish-speaking and about 17,000 as Bulgarian-speaking. It also notes that the Muslim population was administratively concentrated in the three Thracian departments and that the naming of the minority shifted with Greek–Turkish relations.[2]

The identity literature is internally contested. Human Rights Watch described the minority as mixed ethnolinguistically—ethnic Turks, Pomaks and Roma—while reporting that many Pomaks and Roma identified as Turkish in public or political contexts. It also documented Greek policy shifts in the 1950s between “Muslim” and “Turkish” terminology.[13] The Minority Rights Group profile similarly reports previous estimates of roughly 50 percent Turks, 35 percent Pomaks and 15 percent Roma, but explicitly places these estimates in the context of contested self-identification and incomplete official data.[14]

For Atlas purposes, the Turkish-speaking layer should be built from language reports, school and association records, oral histories, press, and historical census categories. A Turkish-language place name, a minority school or a mosque is evidence of a social and administrative network, not automatic proof that every resident of that place was or is ethnically Turkish.

### Pomak / Bulgarian-speaking Muslims

Pomak is a useful but politically charged historical category. The Minority Rights Group profile describes Pomaks as Muslim Slavs whose dialects are usually classified as Bulgarian, concentrated mainly in villages in the Rhodope Mountains. It notes that many Pomaks self-identify as Turks and that Turkish has been the principal language of minority education, while Pomak dialects remain important in local social life.[14]

The French 1920 material is particularly valuable because it separates Turks and Pomaks in the district totals but warns that Bulgarian authorities often counted Pomaks as Bulgarians. Its overall table gives 86,578 Turks and 11,848 Pomaks within the Muslim population, but the article stresses that boundaries and counting practices differed and that the result was the first French-administered census to count Pomaks explicitly.[15] For Gümülcine the table records 39,601 Turks and 2,341 Pomaks; for Xanthi it records 30,438 Turks and 9,507 Pomaks. These are historical district counts, not present-day village totals.

The Bulgarian dissertation abstract adds an important interpretive caution. It argues that Balkan Slavic-speaking Muslim communities were historically formed through religious as well as linguistic processes and that Pomak self-identification was shaped by competing Greek, Turkish and Bulgarian national projects. It describes early Cold War efforts toward Turkishisation and later Greek efforts to differentiate Pomaks from Turks.[10] The Atlas should record these as policy histories and competing classification regimes, not adjudicate a single ethnic origin.

The highest-priority Pomak landscape is the Rhodope mountain belt, especially the Xanthi-side cluster represented administratively today by Myki municipality. Existing scholarship describes Pomaks as living in small mountain settlements, with the largest concentration in Xanthi province.[16] Myki’s 2021 resident population was 14,237, but that figure is a municipality denominator, not a Pomak count.[1] Village-level labels should be verified against Greek, Turkish and Bulgarian sources before publication.

### Muslim Roma / Romani / Athingani

Roma must be mapped separately from both Turkish-speaking and Pomak categories. “Athingani,” “Gypsy,” “Roma” and “Muslim Roma” have not been stable labels. A study of Western Thracian Muslims in Athens records estimates ranging from 5,000 Muslim Roma to 15 percent of the minority, but its main contribution is methodological: Turkish-speaking Muslim Roma may identify as Turks, Turkish-Roma or Roma depending on context, and other minority members may classify them differently.[16]

The same study shows why language cannot stand in for ethnicity. Many Muslim Roma migrants in Athens spoke Turkish or a Turkish–Romani mix and relied on Western Thrace institutions, yet were labelled “Gypsies” by other Western Thracian migrants. The Atlas should therefore use separate fields for **reported language**, **self-identification**, **external classification**, **religious affiliation**, and **source location**. Avoid assigning Roma identity to a settlement solely because a source uses a Muslim-minority estimate.

### Other identities and urban pluralism

The 1919–20 French material records Greeks, Bulgarians, Jews, Armenians, Vlachs, Albanians and Roma in addition to Turks and Pomaks. It concludes that every district contained multiple “nations” and that no clean state boundary could be drawn around religion, language or ethnicity.[15] Gümülcine’s Ottoman and early modern urban record also shows Muslim, Orthodox, Jewish and Armenian households, while Xanthi’s tobacco economy connected local Christians, Muslims, Jews and international merchants.[4][6] These urban pluralities should be retained in the Atlas rather than reduced to a Muslim-versus-Christian binary.

## 4. Lausanne, exchange, settlement and migration

The 1923 exception did not mean that Western Thrace escaped demographic change. The convention exempted the region’s Muslim inhabitants from the compulsory exchange, but Greek-speaking and Orthodox refugees from Asia Minor, Pontus, Eastern Thrace and elsewhere were settled in the region. The Minority Rights Group profile reports around 60,000 Greek-speaking refugees resettled in Western Thrace in the 1920s and records later land reallocations and emigration pressures.[14] Joëlle Dalègre’s French study frames 1922–28 as an emergency reception and difficult cohabitation between Orthodox refugees expelled from Turkey and mostly Turkish-speaking Muslims allowed to remain; 1928 marks the end of the League of Nations’ intervention in the region.[17]

The German Federal Agency for Civic Education estimates that roughly 400,000 Muslims from Greece were affected by the broader exchange, while about 124,000 Muslims of Western Thrace were exempted. These numbers are historical estimates from a secondary synthesis; the treaty itself is the authority for the legal exception.[18] In the Atlas, map the legal exception separately from the actual movements of refugees, emigrants and later return migrants.

The French-administered 1920 census is an unusually strong bridge between legal history and GIS. It reconstructed village sheets and district totals from Bulgarian authorities, French gendarmerie, Muslim communities, municipalities and refugee lists. The overall total was 204,690, with 86,578 Turks, 11,848 Pomaks, 56,114 Bulgarians, 54,092 Greeks, 2,985 Jews, 1,880 Armenians and 3,041 people in other categories. The article cautions that its district boundaries differed from 1906 Ottoman units, that many Muslims had already left in 1913, and that Bulgarian enumerators often folded Pomaks into Bulgarians.[15]

Do not use the French table as an unqualified ethnic base map. The Dedeağaç row is internally difficult in the extracted text, and the author’s own discussion warns about moving populations, boundary changes and counting conventions. The safe use is to digitize the original village sheets and retain the enumerator, date, district, category vocabulary and confidence level for every record.

Post-1923 migration continued in waves. Sule Chousein identifies migration to Turkey during 1940–45 under Bulgarian occupation, 1946–49 during the Greek Civil War, 1952–59 under Turkey’s free-migration policy, in the 1970s after the Cyprus conflict, and later in response to discrimination and economic expectations. The paper cites 23,808 Muslims migrating from Western Thrace to Turkey between 1939 and 1960, with uncertainty around the components of the total, and notes that some Xanthi plain villages were nearly emptied when landlords and then ordinary villagers left.[19] It also describes late-1990s return migration and continuing family networks in Turkey and Germany. These flows are suitable for a migration-network layer, but the Atlas should mark whether a number comes from an archive, a newspaper, fieldwork, or a secondary estimate.

## 5. Settlement and heritage priorities

### Komotini/Gümülcine urban core

Komotini is the strongest urban anchor for Ottoman-to-modern continuity. Priority sites are the Evrenos Bey Imaret, Eski Mosque, Yeni Mosque, clock tower, old market, castle and associated neighbourhoods. The Greek Ministry of Culture dates the central Imaret building to 1364–1382, identifies it as one of the earliest surviving Ottoman monuments in Europe, and records later conversion of part of the complex into an ice factory in 1924 and electricity facilities until 1973.[20] The Egnatia inventory records the Eski Mosque’s early Ottoman phase and later rebuilding, the continuing use of the Yeni Mosque, the 1884/85 clock tower and the city’s Christian, Muslim, Jewish and Armenian urban layers.[4]

For the Atlas, the Imaret should be represented not only as a point of interest but as a biography: Ottoman charitable foundation, Bulgarian-occupation reuse, 1924 industrial conversion, post-1973 heritage intervention and current museum use. Waqf boundaries, cemeteries, fountains and market streets should be mapped where records permit.

### Xanthi/İskeçe urban core

Xanthi’s principal heritage layer is the interaction of Ottoman administration and the tobacco economy. The old town contains Ottoman-era houses, the Muzaffer Bey konak, the clock tower, former market and a preserved neighbourhood fabric. The official Egnatia inventory counts 57 surviving tobacco warehouses from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, including the Ottoman Tobacco Monopoly “Régie” warehouse.[6]

The clock tower was built in 1870 by tobacco merchant Hacı Emin Ağa. The central market mosque was destroyed during the 1941 Bulgarian occupation, while the tower survived. This is a useful example of how the Atlas can map both surviving and lost heritage, with a source image or pre-1941 plan attached to the destroyed site.[6]

### Genisea/Yenice and the tobacco hinterland

Genisea is a high-value village-town case. After its 1870 fire, Xanthi assumed the kaza role. Today Genisea retains the operating Mustafa Pasha Mosque, the non-operating Kasaba Mosque built in 1873 on the site of an earlier mosque, Bektashi tekkes and open-air graves, a historic khan and about twenty tobacco warehouses, most dating from the 1830s–60s. The official inventory also records post-1922 refugee settlement and interwar distribution of former çiftlik lands to landless refugees.[21]

Genisea is useful because it links Ottoman religious institutions, tobacco, Bektashi memory, refugee settlement and land reform in one place. It is a stronger village-level pilot than an unverified list of mountain villages.

### Rhodope mountain and rural clusters

The Atlas should prioritize Myki/Mustafçova and adjacent mountain villages as a Pomak-language and minority-institution research cluster, while keeping the identity label provisional. The evidence supports a strong Rhodope concentration, especially in Xanthi province, but not a clean village-by-village ethnic boundary.[14][16] The OSCE-hosted 2009 document *Mosques in Western Thrace* is an advocacy inventory rather than a neutral census. It lists mosque and minaret counts and names candidate rural sites including Kimmeria, Hemetli, Selino/Kereviz, Soğucak, Okçular, Venna/Demirbeyli, Kallindirio/Kalenderköy, Filira/Sirkeli, Seleron/Gökçeler and Mavromati/Karagozlu.[22]

These names are valuable leads for a mosque, cemetery, vakıf and village-place-name layer. They should not be published as definitive Pomak or Turkish villages without corroboration from municipal records, school registers, oral histories, historical maps and local association sources. The OSCE document also gives regional mosque counts—301 in Western Thrace in 2006, including 161 in the Komotini area, 115 in Xanthi and 25 in Alexandroupoli—but because the document was submitted by a minority association, the counts should be tagged “advocacy inventory, 2006,” pending official verification.[22]

### Alexandroupoli/Dedeağaç

Alexandroupoli is a priority for the modern-port and refugee layers rather than for a dense surviving Ottoman monument layer. The town’s Ottoman growth, railway, port and 1920 transfer to Greece are well documented in municipal and university sources.[7][8] Map the railway station, port, early grid, consular/administrative sites, refugee neighbourhoods and the relationship with nearby Feres, Traianoupoli and Soufli. The present Alexandroupoli municipality was enlarged under the 2011 Kallikratis reform, so do not use its 71,751 residents as a historical Dedeağaç count.[1][8]

## 6. Map/GIS production plan

The region is suitable for a source-aware historical gazetteer. Gkadolou’s Greek place-name project demonstrates the method: a spatial database built from historical maps and reports can carry stable identifiers, alternative names, place type, temporal scope, geometry and links to authority files. Her dataset contains nearly 9,300 geographic entities, including settlements and points of interest, and links historical names to Pandektis, Wikidata, the Getty Thesaurus of Geographic Names and the World Historical Gazetteer.[23]

For Rumelia Atlas, create one stable place identifier for every settlement, neighbourhood, monument, kaza, sanjak, municipality and migration destination. Store Greek, Ottoman Turkish, modern Turkish, Bulgarian, French and English variants separately. Each record should include the source language, source date, name spelling, administrative unit, latitude/longitude, geometry type, confidence and a “not the same place” flag for unresolved homonyms.

Build the following synchronized layers:

- **Administrative time slices:** Ottoman kaza/sanjak; 1906 and 1919–20 district units; interwar prefectures; postwar prefectures; current regional units and municipalities.
- **Population observations:** tahrir, avarız, cizye, salname, French 1920 district/village sheets, 1928 language categories, Greek census and 2021 ELSTAT counts. Preserve the original category vocabulary and denominator.
- **Identity evidence:** self-identification, language, religious institution, external classification and policy classification as separate attributes. Never collapse them into one “ethnicity” field.
- **Heritage:** surviving, altered, reused, demolished and lost mosques, tekkes, waqf properties, cemeteries, churches, synagogues, tobacco warehouses, khans, railway and port structures.
- **Mobility:** 1912–23 displacement, Lausanne exchange/exemption, 1922–28 refugee settlement, 1940s flight, 1950s migration to Turkey, 1970s migration, late-1990s return and continuing diaspora links.
- **Uncertainty:** exact, approximate, inferred, contested and unverified. A map should show uncertainty spatially, not hide it in prose.

The first GIS deliverable should be a three-period comparison: Ottoman/late Ottoman administrative and urban network; French Inter-Allied 1919–20 settlement and population observations; modern ELSTAT/municipal framework. A second deliverable should show the 1923 legal exception and actual refugee and migrant flows. A third should connect Komotini, Xanthi, Genisea, Myki and Dedeağaç/Alexandroupoli heritage and institutional records.

## 7. Production readiness, gaps and next actions

**Ready now:** a credible regional frame; current municipal denominators; Komotini and Xanthi urban history; Dedeağaç’s late Ottoman railway-port story; the Lausanne legal exception; a well-supported explanation of Turkish-speaking, Pomak and Muslim Roma categories; and a first heritage candidate set.

**Ready with qualification:** historical population mapping from the French 1920 material, 1928 language categories, Ottoman registers and later estimates. Every number must retain its administrative unit, date, category and counting regime. The historical figures are not directly comparable across sources.

**Not ready without further work:** a current ethnic or linguistic choropleth; precise village-level Pomak/Roma/Turkish percentages; a definitive mosque count; a complete Ottoman property map; or a single continuous boundary for “the Muslim minority.”

The next research pass should prioritize five actions. First, obtain page images and metadata for the Ottoman State Archives volume and extract its Gümülcine, Dedeağaç and neighbourhood documents. Second, digitize the original French 1919–20 village sheets and the SHAT 4.N.129 file cited by Dalègre, preserving the enumerator and source chain. Third, acquire Greek archive and municipal records from Komotini, Kavala, Xanthi and Alexandroupoli, especially vakıf, school, mufti, property, refugee and land-reform files. Fourth, reconcile historical names against a stable gazetteer and current settlement geometry using the Gkadolou/WHG model. Fifth, add oral history and community sources from Turkish, Pomak, Roma and refugee organizations, with explicit provenance and positionality labels.

The practical editorial rule is simple: **map what a source says, not what a later political category expects it to say**. Western Thrace can support a high-quality Rumelia Atlas chapter if administrative history, heritage, migration and identity are overlaid without being conflated.
