# Greek Macedonia — source and record memo for the Rumelia Atlas

## Usable scope and naming

For the atlas, treat **Ottoman Selanik/Salonica/Selânik as Thessaloniki**, **Siroz/Serez as Serres**, **Vodina/Voden as Edessa**, **Karaferye as Veria**, and **Filorina/Fırınlı as Florina**. Kavala and Drama were linked administratively and economically in the Ottoman period, with Kavala serving as Drama’s port in the TDV account. Do not collapse Ottoman administrative units, modern municipalities, and present-day prefectures into one geometry without recording the time slice.

The most defensible source sequence is: Ottoman tahrir, avârız, tapu and court records; late-Ottoman salname and population material; 1913/1920/1928 Greek statistical publications; League of Nations and Mixed Commission records; and community or monument inventories. Modern city boundaries are not a safe proxy for Ottoman kaza or sancak boundaries.

A central methodological warning is non-negotiable: Ottoman and early twentieth-century enumerations often counted **religion/millet**, while later or foreign enumerators added language, school allegiance, nationality, or political identity. The French INALCO dissertation reviewed here notes that the 1831 material can distinguish Muslims from “rayas,” but cannot recover the ethnic or linguistic sub-groups within those categories. It also documents how “Bulgarophone,” “Macédoslave,” “Slavophone,” “Turk,” and “Pomak” changed meaning with political context. [1] The atlas should therefore retain the source’s original category and add a controlled modern interpretation only in a notes field.

## Evidence that is safe to carry into a first atlas release

### Thessaloniki / Selanik

The Turkish TDV encyclopedia gives unusually useful pre-modern city evidence from Ottoman tahrir material. A c.1502 fragment records 1,680 Muslim households in 37 Muslim quarters, 1,237 Christian households, and two Jewish groups totaling about 2,500 people. The same entry reports a sharp demographic reconfiguration after the settlement of Sephardi Jews: the Muslim share is given as 31% in 1502, approximately 25% in 1519, and 27% in 1530. In the 1568–69 comparison, Muslims are reported at 746 households while the Jewish community had expanded again. These are households and estimates, not modern census persons, and should not be directly compared with the 1900 or 1913 totals. [2]

For the late Ottoman city, the TDV entry quotes Vasil Kınçov’s 1900 figures as 55,000 Jews, 26,000 “Turks,” 16,000 Greeks, 10,000 Bulgarians, 2,500 Catholics and 8,500 “other,” totaling 118,000. “Turks” here is the source’s category; it must not be silently relabeled as Turkish-speaking or ethnically Turkish. The Jewish Community of Thessaloniki gives a different 1900 total of about 173,000, including approximately 80,000 Jews. This discrepancy is useful evidence of competing counting frames, not a reason to select one number without annotation. [2] [3]

The city’s 1912–1923 Muslim history is documented in a Greek university thesis covering administration, religious life, education, professions, guilds, Muslim press, property, intercommunal relations, and migration up to Lausanne. It is a strong lead for extracting primary records, but the repository page is a metadata/abstract record rather than a freely displayed statistical edition. [4] The EIE/100sources project adds a source-backed August 1924 departure register: 203,925 people had departed from the port of Thessaloniki by that point, and the list includes 146 Muslims from Thessaloniki itself. It also preserves two January–February 1925 police lists of Muslims allowed to remain: the first contains 50 Serbian nationals, 20 Albanian nationals, 12 exchangeables, and 7 of unknown nationality; the second contains 42 Albanian nationals, 8 Circassians, 4 Serbs, 1 Ottoman, 1 French national and 1 unknown. These lists are post-exchange residence records, not a census of the Ottoman Muslim population. [5]

For Jewish heritage, the Jewish Museum of Thessaloniki documents the city as a major Sephardi intellectual, printing and commercial center, including the sixteenth-century rabbinic and printing networks, nineteenth-century Ladino press, the 1917 fire, interwar migration, and the destruction of the community during the Holocaust. The page’s 1900 estimate conflicts with the TDV figure noted above and should be preserved as a source-specific estimate. [3] The Greek Archaeological Cadastre also provides an official map layer and a dedicated “Jewish monuments of Greece” story map, useful for heritage discovery but subject to the Ministry’s restrictive reuse terms. [6] [7]

### Kavala

The INALCO dissertation’s 1831 reconstruction places the Kavala kaza at approximately 90.1% Muslim, using the Ottoman religious classification. This is a kaza-level share and cannot be read as a language or ethnicity percentage. The TDV Drama entry confirms Kavala’s role as Drama’s port and records the paired urban-economic development. [1] [8]

For twentieth-century migration, the Macedonian Heritage chapter records 821 applications representing 2,200 people in the combined “Drama–Kavala” area for the Treaty of Neuilly emigration of Slavic-speaking inhabitants to Bulgaria by October 1924. This is a **Slavic-speaking, voluntary/legal emigration track**, not the Lausanne Muslim exchange and should be kept in a separate migration event class. [9]

The Turkish Ministry of Culture portal’s statistical review of Ottoman monuments explicitly includes Kavala among the principal Greek-Macedonian sites visited and inventoried, alongside Selanik, Serez, Drama, Karaferye/Veria, Vodina, and Florina. It is a useful lead to a monument bibliography, but it is a secondary inventory and not a substitute for Greek monument designation records. [10]

### Drama

The TDV encyclopedia supplies a defensible long chronology from Ottoman primary-register references. In 1454–55, Drama is described as a small settlement with 54 Muslim households, 6 Muslim widows, 152 Christian households, 24 Christian unmarried men and 92 Christian widows, totaling a little over 1,000 persons by the entry’s estimate. In 1487, the Muslim population is estimated at about 400 and the Christian population at 650–700. In the first half of the sixteenth century the town reached approximately 1,500 and Muslims were nearly half. By the late sixteenth century the entry records seven Muslim quarters, one Christian quarter and a Jewish community of 12 households; it also notes that conversions contributed 10–15% of the Muslim increase. Evliya Çelebi’s seventeenth-century description gives a town of roughly 3,000, with mosques, mescids, medreses, tekkes, a hamam, market and workshops. These are not interchangeable census rows: they mix households, estimates, and travel description. [8]

The Central Board of Jewish Communities in Greece (KIS) records Jewish presence in Drama from the medieval period onward and describes the late Ottoman/interwar growth of a tobacco-centered community. It identifies arrivals from Thessaloniki and Serres, especially after the 1913 Serres fire, and records approximately 300 Jewish families/1,200 members in a 1925 document, with a synagogue, two cemeteries and a private school. KIS also documents the March 1943 deportation of Drama’s Jews from the Bulgarian occupation zone and the post-war collapse of the community. [11]

The EADD dissertation record by Vasilios Ritzaleos is a stronger academic lead for the Jewish communities of Eastern Macedonia and Thrace. Its declared coverage includes Serres, Drama and Kavala and the period from the mid-nineteenth century to World War II. The repository warns that the dissertation is copyright-protected, so use metadata and citation, not copied text or scans, until permission is checked. [12]

### Serres / Serez / Siroz

The INALCO dissertation’s 1831 table gives the city of Serres as 34.9% Muslim, 4.6% Jewish and 60.5% Rum/Orthodox, with the important caveat that this is a historical religious classification and not a modern ethnicity table. The surrounding kaza and city must be kept distinct. [1]

The EIE/100sources August 1924 register records 5,346 Muslim departures from Serres. This is one of the strongest city-specific migration figures located in the review because the page explains the register’s provenance, departure port, dates and source bibliography. It should be entered as an event record with date, register type and “Muslims departing under the exchange” rather than as an Ottoman population total. [5]

For Jewish history, the Brill Encyclopedia of Jews in the Islamic World entry is an authoritative bibliographic lead by Yitzchak Kerem, but its full text is paywalled. The freely visible record confirms the article identity, DOI and subject. KIS and the EADD thesis provide open institutional routes for documenting movement from Serres to Drama and other eastern Macedonian communities. [11] [12] Do not extract a Serres Jewish population figure from a search snippet or from the Brill abstract alone.

### Florina / Filorina / Fırınlı

The INALCO dissertation reports approximately 51.3% Muslim for the Florina/Filorina kaza in the 1831 religious count. The Ottoman State Archives publication identifies a substantial run of Florina tapu registers covering 1874–1912, including multiple volumes for 1890–1907 and a 1912 volume. This is a concrete archival path for property, neighborhood and household reconstruction, but the catalogue itself does not provide a ready-made ethnic or linguistic population table. [1] [13]

The August 1924 departure register records 1,068 Muslims from Florina. Separately, the Treaty of Neuilly emigration table records 388 applications representing 826 Slavic-speaking people from “Florina–Astoria” by October 1924. These must be stored as different populations and legal tracks. [5] [9]

For later migration, the Macedonian Heritage chapter documents Florina prefectural passport and police records: about 1,000 passports were issued in the first half of 1927, and the text provides small 1929 monthly counts and named-list examples. These records concern overseas and cross-border emigration, not a surviving Ottoman community. [14]

### Edessa / Vodina / Voden

The INALCO dissertation reports approximately 50.7% Muslim for the Edessa/Vodina kaza in 1831, again using a Muslim-versus-raya framework. The August 1924 register records 10 Muslims from Edessa. The separate Neuilly table records 214 applications representing 466 Slavic-speaking people from Edessa by October 1924. The two figures should never be summed or described as one “minority migration.” [1] [5] [9]

Edessa’s Ottoman-era Muslim, Turkish-speaking, Albanian and Slavic-speaking sub-groups remain under-documented in the sources reviewed here. The atlas should use a “research lead only” record pointing to Ottoman tapu/court sources and Greek 1913/1928 publications, not a fabricated community row.

### Veria / Karaferye

The source set confirms Karaferye/Veria as an Ottoman city in the Greek-Macedonian urban network and includes it in the Turkish Ministry’s monument review. The INALCO dissertation covers the Karaferye/Veria kaza in its 1831 comparative framework, but the extracted material did not expose a stable city-level population figure. Do not invent one from a map, secondary table, or current municipality total. [1] [10]

The EIE/100sources register records 1,594 Muslims from Veroia in August 1924. The Neuilly table records 25 applications representing 99 Slavic-speaking people from Veroia by October 1924. These are separate and usable as migration-event records. [5] [9]

For heritage, a municipal tourism page identifies Ottoman-period structures such as the Hünkâr Mosque and the Old Metropolis, but the page is not an adequate legal or scholarly basis by itself. The preferred route is the Greek Archaeological Cadastre and the relevant Ephorate of Imathia, then permission-check any imagery through Ministry terms. [6] [7]

## Population, identity and migration categories — keep them separate

### Muslims as a religious category

Use “Muslim” when the source counts Muslims, Muslims departing, Muslim households, or Muslim residents. The 1923 Lausanne Convention defined the compulsory exchange through religion and nationality: Turkish nationals of Greek Orthodox religion were to leave Turkey and Greek nationals of Muslim religion were to leave Greece, with important exceptions. The German Federal Agency for Civic Education summarizes the convention as applying from 1 May 1923 and describes roughly 388,146 to 400,000 Muslims leaving Greece; the English Macedonian Heritage chapter gives approximately 350,000 Muslims from Greece and about 190,000 Orthodox Christians from Turkey in its regional history synthesis. These are national totals from different statistical frames, not Greek-Macedonia totals. [15] [9]

The Turkish Foreign Ministry hosts the primary convention text, but its HTML extraction is incomplete in this environment. Use the MFA page together with the treaty text in a legal archive or official print edition before quoting article language. [16] The League of Nations Archives catalogue is the preferred next step for Mixed Commission case files, departure registers and property records; the site was blocked by a browser challenge during this review, so no archival item-level claim should be made from it yet. [17]

### Turkish-speaking and “Turk” records

Keep **Turkish-speaking** as a language field and **Turk/Turkish** as the source’s own category. The TDV Selanik entry’s 1900 “26,000 Turks” is not a language census. The French thesis lists Ottoman labels such as Turks, Turkomans, Circassians, Pomaks, Albanophones and Greek-speaking Muslims as categories that appear in different late-Ottoman and Balkan sources, but explicitly warns that the categories are unstable. [1] A production row should therefore require an exact source, year, geography, original label, count unit and an interpretation note.

### Bosniak

No defensible city-specific Bosniak population row was found for Ottoman Thessaloniki, Kavala, Drama, Serres, Florina, Edessa or Veria in the reviewed institutional and academic sources. The 1925 Thessaloniki police lists identify Serbian, Albanian, Circassian and other nationalities, but they do not identify Bosniaks as a separate category. Do not back-project Bosniak identity from “Serb,” “Muslim,” or “Slavic Muslim.” [5]

### Albanian

The post-exchange Thessaloniki police lists are useful for a tightly bounded Albanian record: 20 Albanian nationals in the first list and 42 in the second, among Muslims permitted to remain in the city in early 1925. This is a residence/exception record, not an Ottoman Albanian population estimate. [5] A separate Albanian-speaking Muslim row elsewhere in Greek Macedonia needs a named register, school, consular, military, or community source.

### Slavic Muslim, Pomak and related categories

“Slavic Muslim” is a linguistic-religious analytical category, not a safe substitute for every Muslim source entry. The Columbia/CIAO academic overview defines Pomaks as Bulgarian-speaking Muslims and warns that Pomaks have been variously counted as Turks, Bulgarians, or a separate group; it also distinguishes them from Bosnian Muslim Slavs. [18] The Brill article on Slavic Muslims likewise shows that labels such as Macedonian Muslim, Torbeshi, Turk, Gorani, Bosniak and Pomak can overlap or be contested, and that institutional recognition varies. [19]

For Greek Macedonia, the Macedonian Heritage chapter provides a directly usable **Slavic-speaking** migration series under the Treaty of Neuilly: by October 1924, 22,816 applications/people were distributed across districts, including Thessaloniki 2,206, Veroia 99, Edessa 466, Florina–Astoria 826, Drama–Kavala 2,200 and Serres 761. These are source-defined Slavic-speaking emigrants to Bulgaria; they are not automatically Muslim, Bulgarian, Macedonian, Pomak, or Turkish-speaking. [9]

### Jewish communities

Keep Jewish records separate from religiously aggregated “non-Muslim” or “rayah” totals. Thessaloniki’s Sephardi community had a distinctive Ottoman institutional history, Ladino press and trans-Mediterranean migration. Drama’s Jewish community drew arrivals from Thessaloniki and Serres and was later destroyed in the Bulgarian occupation zone. Serres, Kavala and Drama belong in the Jewish migration and heritage layer even where no stable census row is yet available. [3] [11] [12]

## Heritage and map/GIS assets

1. **Greek Archaeological Cadastre / National Archive of Monuments.** The official Ministry map presents current, authoritative monument, archaeological-site, historic-place and protection-zone information and includes thematic story maps for Jewish monuments. Treat it as a discovery and verification layer. The Ministry’s terms prohibit copying, republication, modification, distribution or commercial reuse of site content without permission; third-party materials remain separately protected. [6] [7]
2. **Turkish Ministry of Culture and Tourism statistical review.** The review is a useful multilingual lead to Ottoman-period monuments across Greek Macedonia, explicitly naming Selanik, Kavala, Serez, Drama, Karaferye/Veria, Vodina and Florina. Use it to seed a bibliography and candidate inventory, then verify every structure against Greek designation records or an academic monument study. [10]
3. **Library of Congress map collections.** The LOC Macedonia guide points to Ottoman-period material under “Turkey in Europe,” a 1885 French *Carte commerciale de la province de Macédoine (Turquie d’Europe)*, ethnographic maps, Balkan maps and map collections covering regional divisions, roads and cities. LOC’s rights page says maps in the digitized single-sheet collection are believed public domain, but requires an item-level legal assessment and credit line; non-digitized or copyrighted maps may require permission. [20] [21]
4. **Wikimedia Commons Rumelia map.** The 1801 Rumelia map from William Miller’s 1913 *The Ottoman Empire* is marked public domain as a faithful reproduction of a two-dimensional public-domain work. The file page also cautions that national rules can differ for photographic reproductions. It is suitable as a contextual base map with full provenance, not as a georeferenced administrative boundary source. [22]
5. **Europeana.** Europeana publishes metadata under CC0, but digital objects must be reused according to the item-level rights statement. If no rights statement is present, check the contributing institution; do not assume that a historical scan is reusable merely because it is on Europeana. [23]
6. **OpenStreetMap.** OSM data are under ODbL. Public atlas maps need readable attribution to OpenStreetMap and a link to the ODbL terms; databases and derivative databases carry additional share-alike obligations. [24]

## Recommended atlas record policy

A production record should contain: `place_id`; modern and Ottoman names; `geometry_type`; `historical_unit`; `valid_from`/`valid_to`; `source_language`; `source_title`; `source_institution`; `source_year`; `original_category`; `count_unit`; `number`; `migration_event`; `legal_basis`; `heritage_status`; `rights_status`; and a short uncertainty note.

Do not publish a detailed population table for the seven cities until each row has an item-level citation and the source’s geography and unit are checked. The present evidence supports a first release of **source-linked event and heritage records**, plus a small number of explicitly qualified historical observations, not a seamless ethnic census series. In particular:

- Do not convert Ottoman “Muslim” to “Turkish.”
- Do not convert “Slavic-speaking” emigrants to “Slavic Muslims.”
- Do not infer Bosniak presence from Serbian, Muslim, or Ottoman nationality labels.
- Do not merge Lausanne Muslim departures with Neuilly Slavic-speaking emigration.
- Do not merge city, kaza, sancak and prefecture totals.
- Do not reuse Ministry images or Archaeological Cadastre content commercially without a rights check and, where required, a licence.

## Gaps requiring targeted archival work

- Obtain and transcribe Greek 1913, 1920 and 1928 statistical publications at city/kaza level, with original Greek category labels and pagination.
- Query Ottoman salname, tahrir, avârız, tapu and court-record catalogues for Kavala, Drama, Serres, Florina, Edessa and Veria; the State Archives publication gives a concrete Florina tapu lead but not the full Greek-Macedonia series. [13]
- Use the League of Nations Mixed Commission files for named departure registers, property liquidation, exemptions and nationality disputes. The online inventory was not accessible during this pass. [17]
- Establish separate evidence for Turkish-speaking, Bosniak, Albanian, Pomak/Bulgarian-speaking Muslim, Greek-speaking Muslim/Valahades and other Slavic Muslim groups. Do not use a single “Turkish/Muslim” field.
- Verify all surviving mosques, hamams, tekkes, synagogues, cemeteries, tobacco warehouses and refugee-settlement sites against the Greek Ministry map and local Ephorate records.
- For map production, georeference only maps with stable edition metadata and preserve the original map as a non-georeferenced scan; record datum, control points, transformation and rights.
