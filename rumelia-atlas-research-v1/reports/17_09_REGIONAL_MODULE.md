# Albania regional memo for Rumelia Atlas (v1)

Albania should be mapped as a **cross-vilayet Ottoman zone**, not as a single stable Ottoman province. The modern state contains parts of the historical Shkodër/İşkodra, Yanya/Janina, Manastır/Monastir and Kosovo administrative worlds; their borders and names changed repeatedly between the fifteenth century and 1912. The most useful atlas unit is therefore a time-stamped place record that stores Ottoman name, modern name, vilayet, sanjak/liva, kaza, nahiye and source date separately.

## 1. Administrative frame and place-name control

The early Ottoman record layer begins with the **Arvanid/Arnavid-ili** tahrir tradition. For the sixteenth century, the Ottoman State Archives’ published catalog identifies BOA TD 94 (H. 926) as an Avlonya (Vlora) liva summary register, TD 99 as its detailed counterpart, and TD 1078/TD 367 as registers covering Avlonya, Belgrad, Argirikasrı (Gjirokastër), Delvine, Yanya, İşkenderiye (Shkodër), Ohri, İlbasan (Elbasan) and related units. These are fiscal-administrative registers, not modern ethnic censuses: they enumerate taxable reaya, timars, fortresses and settlements, and they can identify a village or mahalle without describing a modern national identity. [1]

The Tanzimat-era vilayet system introduced a more regular hierarchy of **vilayet → sanjak/liva → kaza → nahiye → village**, but the hierarchy remained mobile. Şemseddin Sami’s late nineteenth-century *Kâmûsü’l-A‘lâm* treats 68 Albanian cities as distributed among the Yanya, Kosovo, Manastır and İşkodra vilayets, a useful contemporary gazetteer view of “Albanian” geography rather than proof of a single Albanian province. [2] The four-vilayet frame became politically important after 1878, but it should not be drawn as an Ottoman administrative unit that actually existed in the proposed form.

**Shkodër/İşkodra Vilayet.** Gençoğlu dates the creation of the vilayet to late 1867 or early 1868. It initially comprised İşkodra and Zadrima sanjaks; in 1868 it temporarily expanded to include Prizren and Debre, with İlbasan/Elbasan in the Debre grouping, before the boundaries were narrowed again. After the 1877–78 war, the vilayet lost Podgorica, Bar/Antivari and Ulcinj/Ülgün to Montenegro, and its remaining districts were reorganized several times. A 1878 snapshot records 10 kazas, five nahiyes and 645 villages; the 1893 and 1908 schedules show the important coastal-central chain of İşkodra, Lezhë, Puka, Krujë/Akçahisar, Durrës/Draç, Tirana/Tiran, Kavajë/Kavaye and Shijak. [3]

**Yanya/Janina Vilayet.** The southern Albanian interior was linked to the Yanya administrative world through the **Ergiri/Argyrokastro (Gjirokastër) sanjak** and the **Berat/Avlonya** area. The 1878 demographic reconstruction based on British consular reports gives 75,349 Muslim and 88,803 non-Muslim males for the Argyrokastro sanjak, and 109,084 Muslim and 32,202 non-Muslim males for the Berat/Avlonya sanjak. These are sex-specific consular counts, not a total modern population and not directly comparable with INSTAT. [4]

**Manastır/Monastir Vilayet.** Korçë/Görice and eastern Albanian borderlands belonged to the Manastır orbit, while Elbasan shifted into and out of neighboring structures. A late nineteenth-century description identifies Manastır, Debre, Görice, İlbasan and Serfice as five sanjaks. The 1530 Ottoman register layer already separates İlbasan liva from Avlonya and Yanya, demonstrating that a modern city’s “Ottoman province” depends on the year selected. [1] [5]

## 2. City and corridor notes

### Shkodër (İşkodra/İskenderiye) and the northern corridor

Shkodër was the administrative and commercial anchor of the northwestern Ottoman zone. Its record set is unusually rich: Shkodër Vilayet salnames, provincial reorganization studies, AQSH court registers, tax/cadastral material and nineteenth- and early twentieth-century photographs. The 1894 salname table reproduced by Gençoğlu distinguishes Muslim, Catholic and Orthodox households and persons by kaza and nahiye. This makes Shkodër a strong test case for **religion-by-settlement** mapping, but the categories should remain those of the source rather than be converted into ethnicity. [3]

The northern border corridor should be modeled as **Shkodër–Podgorica–Bar/Antivari–Ulcinj–Lezhë**, with a second branch through **Krujë and Durrës**. The 1878 territorial changes make this corridor a moving frontier: places administered from Shkodër before the war were transferred to Montenegro, while populations, officials and Muslim households could remain mobile across the new line. The State Archives publication is useful for Albanian-language administration, appointments and local governance; one document explicitly concerns appointing Albanian-speaking kaymakams in the Durrës-linked kazas. [6]

Shkodër’s communal profile was not simply “Muslim Albanian.” Catholics were highly visible in the 1894 tables, Orthodox communities also appear, and the city’s schools and religious institutions connected it to Adriatic, Italian, Montenegrin and Ottoman networks. A map layer should therefore distinguish **Muslim Albanian households**, **Catholic Albanian or local Catholic communities where the source permits**, **Orthodox communities**, and **muhacir/migrant households**, without assuming that language, religion and ethnicity coincide.

### Tirana, Durrës, Kavajë, Shijak and the central corridor

Tirana was a relatively late Ottoman urban formation compared with Berat, Durrës or Shkodër. Kavalçalan’s study, using salnames and historical photographs, associates the city’s growth with Süleyman Paşa’s early seventeenth-century mosque, imaret, han and hamam. The 1896/97 Shkodër Vilayet salname records six mosques, 32 mescids, 15 tekkes, 32 zaviyes, a medrese, schools, two Orthodox churches and a Catholic church. This is valuable for reconstructing a **multi-institutional Muslim-majority urban node**, but it does not establish a Turkish ethnic majority. [7]

The central road corridor is **Durrës–Kavajë–Tirana–Shijak–Peklin–Elbasan**, with branches toward Krujë and the interior. The 1880 archival document in the Ottoman State Archives volume records appointments for Tiran, Kavaye, Şiyak and Peklin, while another item concerns repairing and building the Durrës–Kavajë–Elbasan road and improving schools. These records are especially useful for linking administrative geography to transport, education and linguistic competence. [6]

Durrës/Draç was a port and sanjak center whose importance rose and fell with Ottoman coastal strategy. The same archive publication includes material on making Durrës a sanjak, the appointment of officials who knew Albanian and the administration of its dependent kazas. Durrës should be mapped as an Adriatic gateway, not only as a city point: its connections ran inland to Kavajë and Elbasan and seaward toward Italian ports. [6]

Elbasan/İlbasan was both an early Ottoman liva and a later nineteenth-century hinge between central Albania and Manastır. Its court-record series begins with 1529 copies and is held within the wider Yanya–Manastır–İşkodra collection; the archival literature identifies Berat records as continuing to 1926 and the Elbasan sequence as an early starting point. [8] The city’s Ottoman fabric is a useful heritage layer, but it must be read against later demolition and modernization. The Italian urban study places Elbasan within a group of cities where twentieth-century projects were inserted into, or layered over, older bazaar, mosque and fortification structures. [9]

### Berat, Vlora and the south-central Adriatic

Berat/Belgrad-ı Arnavud is one of the best documented places for connecting administration, religious communities and built heritage. The Berat kadi registers begin in 1602 and consist of 211 files in the AQSH description; they are a core source for property, litigation, endowments, family and commercial history. [8] The ICOMOS nomination describes Ottoman expansion beyond the castle into Mangalem and Gorica, sixteenth-century mosques, the late eighteenth-century Helveti tekke, çardak houses and the Ottoman-period Gorica bridge. It also emphasizes the long coexistence of Muslim and Orthodox communities. [10]

Berat should therefore have separate heritage and social layers: **castle/administrative core**, **Muslim charitable and devotional institutions**, **Orthodox ecclesiastical and artistic sites**, **residential-commercial quarters**, and **river/road crossings**. A “Muslim city” label alone would erase the mixed urban record that the ICOMOS documentation makes visible.

Vlora/Avlonya was a maritime and military node linked to Berat, Gjirokastër, Delvinë and the Ionian coast. The Ottoman defter catalog identifies Avlonya liva records that include Avlonya, Belgrad and Argirikasrı kazas, while related registers contain Delvine, Përmet, Tepelenë and other southern units. [1] The 1878 table places Vallona/Avlonya kaza inside the Berat/Avlonya sanjak and counts 21,000 Muslim and 1,400 non-Muslim males there; this figure should be treated as a source-specific consular snapshot. [4]

The Italian urban-history dissertation is useful for mapping how early twentieth-century projects reworked Ottoman urban structure in Vlora, Berat, Durrës, Shkodër, Korçë, Tirana and Elbasan. Its atlas distinguishes “juxtaposition,” “insertion” and “stratification” in relation to the Ottoman city, and it reproduces plans that can be georeferenced as historical urban footprints. [9]

### Gjirokastër, Përmet, Tepelenë and the Janina/Epirus border corridor

Gjirokastër/Ergiri/Argyrokastro was the center of a southern inland sanjak tied to Yanya. It should be mapped with **Përmet/Premedi, Tepelenë/Tepedelen, Delvinë/Delvine, Sarandë and the Janina–Epirus route**, rather than as an isolated city. Sami Frashëri’s biographical and gazetteer context is particularly relevant: he was born at Frashër in the Përmet kaza of the Ergiri sanjak, Yanya Vilayet. [2] [11]

The ICOMOS documentation treats Gjirokastra together with Berat as a rare surviving example of Ottoman-period Balkan urban character. Its stone tower houses, streets, castle and neighborhood morphology should be recorded as an urban landscape, not reduced to individual monuments. [10] The southern corridor also requires a border note: Ottoman administration, Greek Orthodox ecclesiastical networks, Albanian-speaking Muslim communities and movement toward Janina overlapped spatially. The 1878 Argyrokastro counts show a much more mixed Muslim/non-Muslim profile than the Berat/Avlonya sanjak, but the consular categories do not identify all non-Muslims by language or ethnicity. [4]

Bektashi sites belong here and in the Tepelenë–Berat–Elbasan network. The order expanded in late eighteenth-century Albania under Ali Paşa Tepedelenli’s patronage and spread through tekkes; it later acquired political associations in the late Ottoman and post-Ottoman period. [12] A Bektashi tekke should be tagged as a **religious institution with a historical network**, not as a synonym for “Bektashi Albanian” or “Turkish.”

### Korçë/Görice and the eastern border corridor

Korçë/Görice was an eastern Albanian urban and commercial node in the Manastır orbit, connected to **Manastır/Bitola, Florina, Prespa, Kastoria and the Aegean routes**. The Ottoman defter catalog contains Görice and related eastern units in the fiscal record system, while the Manastır salname collection at the Institute for Mediterranean Studies provides nine provincial almanacs for 1875–1896. [1] [13]

Korçë is essential for a migration layer. Dahinden notes that Orthodox young men from southern towns, especially Korçë, migrated to Boston and New York in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; transnational associations and remittances helped build schools, roads and bridges and contributed to Albanian national mobilization. [14] This should be represented as **Orthodox Albanian-speaking migration and transnational institution-building**, not collapsed into a Muslim or Ottoman category.

The 1878 Manastır material records a mixed regional population including Muslims, Rum/Greek, Bulgarians, Vlachs/Aromanians, Serbs and Roma in different kazas. It is a reminder that the eastern corridor’s “Albanian” geography was interwoven with Slavic, Vlach and Greek networks. [4] Current INSTAT categories also identify Aromanian, Greek, Macedonian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Bosnian, Roma and Egyptian identities, but these modern self-identification categories cannot be projected backward onto Ottoman registers without local corroboration. [15]

## 3. Community categories: what can and cannot be separated

**Albanian Muslim.** This is a useful atlas category only when the source provides both an Albanian ethnolinguistic or local identity and a Muslim affiliation. Ottoman records commonly give religion, household status, occupation, place and legal standing; they do not consistently give “Albanian” as a modern ethnicity. The Cambridge synthesis describes Islamized Albanians as major military and administrative actors, while also stressing regional variation, seventeenth-century Islamization and coexistence with Catholic and Orthodox communities. [16]

Schmitt’s German-language survey reinforces the geographic caution: Islamization unfolded unevenly, with a war-damaged Catholic north and a more continuously Orthodox south, while Bektashi and other dervish networks require separate institutional mapping. This is a historiographical synthesis, not a replacement for district-level registers. [20]

**Turkish-speaking.** Do not infer Turkish ethnicity from Ottoman Turkish documents, Muslim names, or service in the Ottoman administration. The strongest verified evidence in this research concerns **Muslim Roma who were often partly or fully Turkish-speaking**, sometimes called “Turkish Gypsies” in surrounding societies. The Council of Europe source warns that these communities were internally diverse and could combine Turkish and Romani or lose Romani over time. [17] Local Ottoman records may also mention muhacirs or officials with language competence, but those labels require a source-specific transcription and should not be normalized as “Turks.”

**Roma.** The Council of Europe synthesis identifies Ottoman fiscal registers for kıptı/çingene populations and reports 374 Roma households in the territory corresponding to present Albania in the 1522–23 Roumelia register. It also records Mečkara communities present for centuries and later Kabudži/Kalburdži, Kurtofja and Arlia groups, plus Sepedži who arrived from Asia Minor through Greece after the 1923 population exchange. [17] These figures and group names are valuable leads, but they are not a complete census of Roma in all Albanian districts.

**Bektashi.** Bektashism is an order and religious affiliation, not an ethnicity or language. The 2023 INSTAT census separately records 115,644 “Muslim–Bektashi” residents alongside 1,101,718 “Muslim” residents. [15] This is a current self-identification instrument, not an Ottoman historical count. For the Ottoman layer, map tekkes, dedications, waqf/endowment ties, patrons and itineraries; do not translate a tekke’s presence into a population number.

**Other categories.** Orthodox, Catholic, Vlach/Aromanian, Greek, Slavic, Jewish, Egyptian and mixed or non-denominational identities should remain visible where sources support them. INSTAT’s 2023 questionnaire explicitly separates ethnicity, religion and language and permits “prefer not to answer” and unavailable responses. Its tables list 9,813 Roma, 12,375 Egyptian, 23,485 Greek and 2,459 Aromanian residents, among other groups; these are modern identities and should be stored as a separate temporal layer. [15]

## 4. Source catalog for the Rumelia Atlas

### Ottoman archival records

1. **BOA tahrir and muhasebe registers:** TD 94, TD 99, TD 1078 and TD 367 for Avlonya, Berat/Belgrad, Argirikasrı, Delvine, Yanya, İşkodra, Ohri and İlbasan. Use for villages, taxable households, revenues, timars, fortresses and administrative geography; preserve folio/page references and Ottoman spelling. [1]
2. **AQSH / Central State Archive of Albania:** court registers of Yanya, Manastır and İşkodra vilayets; Elbasan copies begin in 1529, Berat registers begin in 1602 and the described sequence runs to 1926. Use for mahkeme, inheritance, property, waqf and commercial networks. [8]
3. **Ottoman State Archives publication, *Osmanlı Arşiv Belgelerinde Arnavutluk / Shqipëria në dokumentet arkivale osmane*:** bilingual document images and translations on Durrës, Tirana, Kavajë, Shijak, Peklin, Elbasan, Avlonya, education, roads, taxation and politics. Use as a finding aid and published primary-source sample, not as a substitute for the full BOA file. [6]
4. **Provincial salnames:** the IMS/FORTH collection holds eight Yanya volumes for 1871–1901 and nine Manastır volumes for 1875–1896; Shkodër Vilayet salnames are cited in the Turkish architectural literature and in the Shkodër administrative study. Use salnames for institutions, officials, schools, roads, population tables and place-name crosswalks, recording Hijri/Rumi/Gregorian dates. [3] [7] [13]

### Gazetteers and censuses

- **Şemseddin Sami, *Kâmûsü’l-A‘lâm* (1888–1898/99):** late Ottoman Turkish historical-geographical encyclopedia; the Albanian-city study identifies 68 detailed city entries across the four vilayet frame. Use as a contemporary gazetteer, while flagging authorial and nationalist framing. [2] [11]
- **1878 British consular demographic reports:** useful for a consistent male-population snapshot of Yanya, Berat/Avlonya, Argyrokastro and Manastır areas, but categories are consular and often combine religious or “millet” labels with geography. [4]
- **INSTAT Albania 2023 Population and Housing Census:** use only for the modern comparison layer. It records 2,402,113 residents; 1,101,718 Muslim, 115,644 Muslim–Bektashi, 201,530 Catholic and 173,645 Orthodox; 9,813 Roma, 12,375 Egyptian, 23,485 Greek and 2,459 Aromanian by ethnicity; 2,187,788 report Albanian as the usual home language. The census itself cautions that ethnicity, religion and language are distinct, subjective dimensions. [15]

### Maps, GIS and heritage

- **GIS method:** Aladağ’s open Zenodo paper demonstrates geocoding Ottoman kaza points, drawing vilayet/sanjak polygons and tracking administrative mobility from tahrir and kadıasker registers. It is a method reference; its early sixteenth-century empire-wide model should be rebuilt with Albanian-specific source layers. [18]
- **Historical urban plans:** Resta’s Italian dissertation supplies an atlas of Tirana, Vlora, Shkodër, Korçë, Durrës, Berat and Elbasan and reads early twentieth-century interventions against Ottoman bazaars, mosques, fortifications and street fabrics. Use its plans as secondary georeferencing leads and verify against archival plan inventories. [9]
- **Photographic and visual evidence:** Kavalçalan’s study reconstructs demolished Ottoman buildings through photographs, salnames and foreign travel archives. It is particularly useful for Tirana, Kavajë, Gjirokastër, Durrës, Shkodër and Elbasan, but photographs need date, photographer, viewpoint and building-identity confidence fields. [7]
- **Heritage:** the ICOMOS/UNESCO nomination for Berat and Gjirokastra is the strongest concise heritage source in this set for Ottoman urban morphology, religious coexistence, mosques, tekkes, bridges, houses and historic neighborhoods. [10]
- **Archive discovery:** EHRI identifies AQSH, the General Directorate of Archives and the Institute of History in Tirana, while warning that Albanian archival descriptions remain incomplete. Italian and German collections should be queried for consular, military, cartographic and photographic records. [19]

## 5. Migration and border-corridor layer

The atlas should treat migration as a permanent regional process rather than as a post-1912 exception. Dahinden identifies post-Skanderbeg movement to southern Italy, Dalmatia and Greece; seasonal and internal Ottoman mobility; deportations and elite military careers; the nineteenth-century growth of Albanian populations in Kosovo and Macedonia; and Korçë-linked Orthodox migration to Boston and New York. [14]

For the **north**, model Shkodër–Podgorica–Bar/Ulcinj–Lezhë and Shkodër–Krujë–Durrës as a frontier and Adriatic corridor. For the **center**, model Durrës–Kavajë–Tirana–Shijak–Peklin–Elbasan and the eastward connection toward Manastır. For the **south**, model Berat–Vlora–Tepelenë–Gjirokastër–Përmet–Delvinë–Janina, with maritime links from Vlora and Durrës to Italy and Greece. For the **east**, model Korçë–Prespa–Florina–Manastır–Kastoria and the associated Vlach, Greek, Slavic, Roma and Albanian circuits.

Roma mobility should be a separate layer. The Council of Europe account connects Muslim Roma to Ottoman conquest-era auxiliary, artisan and fiscal systems, later intra-Balkan migration, the 1923 Greece–Turkey population exchange and twentieth-century movement. [17] The category should not be absorbed into either Albanian Muslim or Turkish-speaking populations; where a source records both, store both attributes with provenance.

## 6. Production guidance and remaining gaps

For every atlas feature, store: modern name; Ottoman spellings and aliases; coordinates; date or date range; vilayet; sanjak/liva; kaza; nahiye; settlement type; source genre; folio/page; religion category; language category; ethnic/self-identification category; migration tag; heritage status; and confidence. Keep **source categories** separate from **analyst-normalized categories**.

The strongest high-confidence anchors are the BOA defter catalog, Shkodër administrative study, AQSH kadi-register description, ICOMOS Berat/Gjirokastra nomination, INSTAT 2023 tables, IMS salname holdings and the bilingual Ottoman State Archives publication. The weakest areas are city-level Turkish-speaking populations, precise Ottoman Roma distribution after 1522–23, full kaza-level Korçë statistics, and georeferenced boundaries for each salname year. These should be marked “requires archival verification,” not filled by modern municipal boundaries.
